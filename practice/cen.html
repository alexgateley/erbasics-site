Question 1 · Cardiovascular

A patient with NSTEMI (troponin rising, 2 mm ST depression in V3–V6) continues to have 8/10 chest pain despite sublingual and IV nitroglycerin, metoprolol, aspirin, a P2Y12 inhibitor, and heparin. BP is 108/66 mm Hg, and there are frequent runs of nonsustained VT. Which intervention is MOST appropriate?

  1. Immediate invasive strategy, with angiography within 2 hours
  2. Add IV morphine and continue medical therapy for 48 hours
  3. Stabilize for 24 hours and then perform stress testing
  4. Give alteplase for reperfusion

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. Refractory angina and electrical instability are very-high-risk features requiring an immediate invasive strategy.
  • B. Morphine may mask ischemia and delay P2Y12 absorption without treating the culprit lesion.
  • C. A conservative, ischemia-guided approach is for low-risk patients, not those with ongoing ischemia.
  • D. Fibrinolytics do not benefit NSTE-ACS and increase bleeding.

In NSTE-ACS, timing of the invasive strategy depends on risk. Very-high-risk features call for an immediate strategy (under 2 hours): refractory angina, hemodynamic instability or cardiogenic shock, life-threatening arrhythmias, mechanical complications, acute heart failure, or recurrent dynamic ST changes. High-risk patients (rising troponin, dynamic ST changes, high GRACE score) should undergo an early invasive strategy within 24 hours. Fibrinolytics are contraindicated in NSTE-ACS.

Sources: Jacobson, CV Nursing Practice, 3rd ed.; ACC/AHA ACS Guideline (2025)

Question 2 · Cardiovascular

A patient with atrial fibrillation who stopped taking warfarin 2 months ago develops sudden pain in the left foot. The foot is pale and cool, with absent popliteal and pedal Doppler signals, decreased sensation in the toes, and mild weakness of toe dorsiflexion. Which intervention is the priority?

  1. Start cilostazol for peripheral arterial disease
  2. Elevate the limb above heart level to reduce swelling
  3. IV heparin and emergent vascular surgery consult
  4. Apply a heating pad to improve circulation

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Cilostazol treats chronic claudication, not acute embolic occlusion.
  • B. Elevation further reduces arterial inflow to an ischemic limb. The limb should be kept level or slightly dependent.
  • C. Correct. Sensory loss with weakness indicates an immediately threatened limb that needs anticoagulation and urgent revascularization.
  • D. An ischemic, insensate limb can be burned, and heat increases metabolic demand.

Acute limb ischemia from cardiac embolism presents with the 6 Ps: pain, pallor, pulselessness, poikilothermia, paresthesia, and paralysis. Sensory loss plus motor weakness indicates an immediately threatened (Rutherford IIb) limb requiring revascularization within hours by embolectomy or catheter-directed therapy. The nurse should anticipate an immediate heparin bolus and infusion, keep the limb level and protected, perform frequent neurovascular checks, and watch for reperfusion injury and compartment syndrome afterward.

Sources: Creager, Vascular Medicine, 3rd ed.; Urden, Critical Care Nursing, 9th ed.

Question 3 · Respiratory

A 79-year-old nursing home resident with dysphagia from a prior stroke choked on food 2 days ago and now has fever, a productive cough and a new right lower lobe infiltrate. Data are: T 38.7°C (101.7°F), HR 104/min, RR 26/min, BP 128/74 mm Hg, SpO₂ 92% on 2 L/min. CT shows no cavity, abscess or effusion. There has been no hospital stay or IV antibiotic in the past 90 days and no prior MRSA or Pseudomonas isolate. The patient will be admitted to a medical unit. Which initial antibiotic regimen is MOST consistent with the ATS/IDSA guideline?

  1. Ceftriaxone and azithromycin plus metronidazole
  2. Clindamycin alone for oral anaerobes
  3. Vancomycin plus piperacillin-tazobactam
  4. Ceftriaxone plus azithromycin

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. Adding anaerobic coverage feels logical after a witnessed choking episode, but the guideline suggests not routinely adding it unless lung abscess or empyema is suspected, and imaging shows neither.
  • B. Clindamycin is a traditional aspiration choice, but it is not a recommended pneumonia regimen on its own, and the guideline notes its frequent link to Clostridioides difficile infection.
  • C. Nursing home residence once defined health care-associated pneumonia and triggered broad coverage, but that category has been abandoned; MRSA or Pseudomonas coverage is added only for validated risk factors, which this patient lacks.
  • D. Correct. A beta-lactam plus a macrolide is a standard regimen for nonsevere inpatient pneumonia without MRSA or Pseudomonas risk factors, and suspected aspiration alone does not change it.

The 2019 ATS/IDSA guideline recommends a beta-lactam such as ceftriaxone plus a macrolide, or a respiratory fluoroquinolone alone, for adults admitted with nonsevere community-acquired pneumonia who have no risk factors for MRSA or Pseudomonas aeruginosa. It suggests not routinely adding anaerobic coverage for suspected aspiration pneumonia unless a lung abscess or empyema is suspected, because modern studies show that anaerobes play a small role and added agents such as clindamycin raise the risk of C. difficile. It also recommends abandoning the old health care-associated pneumonia category: living in a nursing home does not by itself justify vancomycin and an antipseudomonal drug, which are reserved for prior respiratory isolation of those organisms or locally validated risk factors such as recent hospitalization with IV antibiotics. The nurse gives the first doses promptly, keeps the head of the bed raised and the patient on nothing by mouth until a swallow screen, and reports any worsening oxygenation.

Sources: Merck Manual, Professional Version; ATS/IDSA Community-Acquired Pneumonia in Adults (2019)

Question 4 · Respiratory

A 4-year-old had 2 days of runny nose and a barky cough, then over the past 6 hours developed high fever and noisy breathing. Data are: T 39.9°C (103.8°F), HR 170/min, RR 40/min, SpO₂ 92% on room air. The child looks toxic, has inspiratory stridor and coughs up thick purulent secretions, but is not drooling and can swallow. Twenty minutes after nebulized epinephrine and dexamethasone, there is no improvement. Which condition should the nurse suspect?

  1. Severe viral laryngotracheitis
  2. Bacterial tracheitis
  3. Acute epiglottitis
  4. Aspirated airway foreign body

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. The barky cough and viral prodrome fit croup, but croup seldom looks toxic, does not produce thick purulent secretions, and usually improves soon after nebulized epinephrine.
  • B. Correct. A viral prodrome followed by abrupt high fever, a toxic appearance, copious purulent secretions and stridor that does not respond to nebulized epinephrine points to bacterial tracheitis, most often from Staphylococcus aureus or group A streptococci.
  • C. Epiglottitis also causes high fever, toxicity and stridor, but it typically brings drooling, dysphagia and a muffled voice without the barking cough, and this child swallows and coughs freely.
  • D. A foreign body can cause stridor that fails to respond to epinephrine, but it starts with a sudden choking episode rather than a viral prodrome followed by high fever.

Bacterial tracheitis is a secondary bacterial infection of the trachea, often after a viral illness such as influenza, that produces thick purulent secretions able to obstruct the airway. Merck describes a markedly toxic child with high fever, copious secretions and rapidly progressing stridor, and states that lack of response to nebulized epinephrine should immediately raise the diagnosis. Management puts the airway first: the nurse prepares for intubation in a controlled setting by a clinician skilled in pediatric airways and for broad IV antibiotics, such as vancomycin with ceftriaxone, while keeping the child calm with a parent and on continuous monitoring. Direct laryngoscopy confirms the diagnosis.

Sources: Merck Manual, Professional Version; AAFP: Croup: Diagnosis and Management (2018)

Question 5 · Neurological

A client with a spinal cord injury at C5 is admitted 2 hours after the injury with a heart rate of 44, blood pressure 80/46, and warm dry skin. Which condition does the nurse recognize?

  1. Neurogenic shock from loss of sympathetic tone
  2. Septic shock from aspiration pneumonia
  3. Autonomic dysreflexia from bladder distension
  4. Hypovolemic shock from internal bleeding

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. Cervical injury interrupts sympathetic outflow, causing bradycardia, hypotension and vasodilation with warm skin.
  • B. Sepsis does not develop within 2 hours and would cause tachycardia.
  • C. Dysreflexia causes hypertension and occurs after spinal shock resolves, not 2 hours after injury.
  • D. Hypovolemia produces tachycardia and cool skin.

Neurogenic shock follows injuries at T6 or above: bradycardia, hypotension, warm flushed skin and poikilothermia. Treatment is fluids, vasopressors, atropine for bradycardia and temperature control. Spinal shock, by contrast, is the temporary loss of reflexes below the injury.

Bottom line: Bradycardia, hypotension and warm dry skin after a cervical or high thoracic cord injury indicate neurogenic shock from loss of sympathetic tone.

Sources: Ignatavicius, Med-Surg Nursing, 11th ed.; Lewis's Medical-Surgical Nursing, 13th ed.

Question 6 · Neurological

A 61-year-old woman was talking with her husband 70 minutes ago when her left arm and leg suddenly became weak. In the ED her NIHSS is 9, a noncontrast CT shows no hemorrhage, and the stroke team is deciding whether to give tenecteplase. Data are: BP 166/92 mm Hg, HR 90, glucose 138 mg/dL. While the team reviews the CT, the nurse finishes the history with her husband. Which detail is MOST important to report before the lytic decision?

  1. She took her usual aspirin 81 mg with breakfast this morning
  2. She had a transient ischemic attack 3 years ago
  3. She had a right knee replacement 6 months ago
  4. Her left arm jerked rhythmically for about a minute before it went weak

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. Tempting because aspirin adds to bleeding risk, but Merck's exclusion table names anticoagulants (an INR above 1.7, treatment-dose low molecular weight heparin within 24 hours, a direct oral anticoagulant within 48 hours), not aspirin. It belongs on the medication list but does not change the decision.
  • B. Merck excludes a stroke or severe head trauma within the past 3 months. An old TIA is a reason for secondary prevention later, not a reason to withhold the lytic now.
  • C. Recent surgery does matter, which is why this is tempting, but Merck's relative exclusion is major surgery or serious trauma within the past 14 days. A joint replacement 6 months ago is far outside that window.
  • D. Correct. Merck lists a seizure at onset with postictal residual deficits as a relative exclusion, because weakness after a seizure can be a postictal deficit rather than an infarct. The stroke team needs this detail before the bolus.

Before a lytic, the nurse's history targets the items on the exclusion list. Merck's tPA table includes absolute exclusions such as any history of intracranial hemorrhage, a stroke or severe head trauma within 3 months, a platelet count below 100,000/mcL and anticoagulant effect, and relative exclusions such as rapidly improving symptoms, major surgery or serious trauma within 14 days, and a seizure at onset with postictal residual deficits. A witnessed seizure matters because postictal weakness can mimic a stroke, and the team has to judge whether the deficit is ischemic. The 2026 AHA/ASA guideline, as quoted by Boettger, calls for IV thrombolysis as rapidly as possible in eligible patients, so the nurse reports the seizure at once rather than at handoff.

Sources: Merck Manual, Professional Version; AHA/ASA Acute Ischemic Stroke (2026); Boettger: 2026 AHA/ASA Stroke Guidelines, Global Perspective (2026)

Question 7 · Gastrointestinal

A 45-year-old has had constant right upper quadrant pain for 4 hours that began after a fried meal. During deep palpation below the right costal margin, she abruptly stops inhaling. Data are: T 37.9°C (100.2°F), HR 96, BP 138/84 mm Hg, WBC 12,400/mm³, lipase normal. Which plan should the nurse anticipate?

  1. A HIDA scan first, avoiding morphine because it causes sphincter of Oddi spasm
  2. An upright chest x-ray and an oral antacid for a probable peptic ulcer
  3. CT of the abdomen first, with analgesia held until the surgeon examines her
  4. Right upper quadrant ultrasound and IV ketorolac

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. Cholescintigraphy is reserved for equivocal ultrasound or suspected acalculous disease, and opioids are acceptable for biliary pain when needed.
  • B. An upright film looks for free air from a perforated ulcer, but postprandial right upper quadrant pain with a positive Murphy sign, fever and leukocytosis points to the gallbladder.
  • C. Holding analgesia to preserve the examination is an outdated belief, and CT is less sensitive than ultrasound for gallstones; it is reserved for unclear diagnoses or complications.
  • D. Correct. Ultrasound is the best test to detect gallstones and signs of cholecystitis, and an NSAID such as ketorolac is effective analgesia for biliary pain.

Pain beyond a few hours with fever, leukocytosis and a positive Murphy sign (inspiratory arrest when the inflamed gallbladder meets the palpating hand) suggests acute cholecystitis rather than simple biliary colic. Merck names transabdominal ultrasound the best test to detect gallstones; it also shows wall thickening, pericholecystic fluid and a sonographic Murphy sign, and it uses no radiation or contrast. Cholescintigraphy (HIDA) is kept for equivocal results or suspected acalculous disease. Analgesia should not be delayed: Merck lists an NSAID such as ketorolac or an opioid for analgesia, so neither the examination nor a myth about the sphincter of Oddi justifies leaving her in pain. Management then includes NPO status, IV fluids, antibiotics and early laparoscopic cholecystectomy.

Sources: Merck Manual Professional: Acute Cholecystitis (2025); Sheehy's Emergency Nursing, 7th ed.

Question 8 · Genitourinary, Gynecology and Obstetrics

A 15-year-old soccer goalkeeper was kicked in the scrotum 3 hours ago. The right hemiscrotum is swollen, bruised and tender, and pain is 8/10 despite ice. Data are: HR 104/min, BP 128/74 mm Hg. Scrotal ultrasound shows a break in the tunica albuginea, a heterogeneous right testis and an intratesticular hematoma, with Doppler flow present. Which plan should the nurse anticipate?

  1. Urgent surgical exploration and repair
  2. Admission for bed rest and ice because Doppler flow is preserved
  3. Manual detorsion followed by elective fixation of both testes
  4. Scrotal support, NSAIDs and repeat ultrasound in 48 to 72 hours

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. A break in the tunica albuginea is testicular rupture, and guidelines recommend urgent exploration with closure of the tunica, or orchidectomy if the testis cannot be saved.
  • B. Preserved flow is reassuring when torsion is the question, but it does not show that the tunica is intact; the extruding, injured tubules still need repair.
  • C. Manual detorsion is a bridge for torsion when surgery is delayed. This testis has blood flow and a ruptured capsule, so rotating it treats the wrong problem and adds pain.
  • D. Support and analgesia suit a contusion with an intact tunica. With a breach, waiting days raises the risk of losing the testis: delaying surgery by 72 hours has been linked to an orchidectomy rate of 45% against 9% after acute exploration.

Most pediatric scrotal injuries are blunt, from sports kicks and projectiles, bicycle straddle injuries, crashes and assaults, which compress the testis against the pubic bone. Ultrasound with color Doppler characterizes the injury: disruption of the tunica albuginea, especially with loss of homogeneity or an intratesticular hematoma, marks a severe injury. Merck classifies a testicular injury with a disrupted tunica albuginea as a rupture that needs surgical exploration and repair, and EAU and AUA guidance cited in a 2025 scoping review recommend urgent exploration. In that review, testicular atrophy followed 5.2% of acute repairs, 15.5% of conservative courses and 35.7% of delayed repairs. Ultrasound can miss a fracture early, so a high index of suspicion and examination still matter.

Sources: Merck Manual, Professional Version; Merck Manual: Testicular Torsion (2025); Wong's Care of Infants & Children, 12th ed.

Question 9 · Mental Health

A 2-year-old has partial-thickness scald burns to both feet and ankles. The father says the child climbed into the bathtub by himself while hot water was running. Which assessment finding MOST strongly suggests the burns were inflicted?

  1. Burns of uniform depth with sharp, symmetric borders and no splash marks
  2. Irregular burns of varying depth with drip marks down the shins
  3. Arrival 20 minutes after the injury with the feet wrapped in wet towels
  4. A father who becomes tearful and anxious while giving the history

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. A limb held still in hot water burns evenly up to a sharp, matching water line in a stocking pattern, which a child scrambling in the water does not produce.
  • B. This is the splash pattern of a child moving in or out of hot water, which fits the father’s account rather than contradicting it.
  • C. Delay in seeking care is a red flag, but prompt arrival after cool-water first aid is what a caregiver of an accidentally burned child would do.
  • D. Distress is common in caregivers of accidentally injured children and is not a reliable sign of abuse; the burn pattern is more informative than demeanor.

Forced immersion produces burns of uniform depth with sharp, symmetric lines of demarcation in a stocking or glove distribution, often without splash marks because the limb is held still. Flexed skin creases may be spared (“zebra stripes”), and the center of the buttocks may be spared where it pressed against the cooler tub. A child who climbs or falls into hot water moves and splashes, which gives irregular margins, variable depth and drip marks. Other red flags are isolated burns of the buttocks or perineum, a story that does not fit the child’s development and delayed care. A suspicious pattern is reported to child protective services.

Sources: Nonaccidental Trauma Review, Cureus (2022); ENA ENPC Provider Manual, 6th ed.; Wong's Care of Infants & Children, 12th ed.; Sheehy's Emergency Nursing, 7th ed.

Question 10 · Medical

A client with Addison disease is admitted with vomiting, weakness, hypotension and a serum sodium of 126 mEq/L. Which intervention should the nurse expect?

  1. IV hydrocortisone and dextrose in normal saline
  2. Insulin infusion for the stress response
  3. Potassium chloride supplementation for the vomiting losses
  4. Fluid restriction to correct the low sodium level

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. Adrenal crisis is treated with glucocorticoid replacement and volume and glucose correction.
  • B. These clients tend toward hypoglycemia.
  • C. Addison crisis causes hyperkalemia; potassium is not given.
  • D. The client is volume depleted; restriction worsens shock.

Adrenal insufficiency lacks cortisol and aldosterone: hyponatremia, hyperkalemia, hypoglycemia, hypotension, fatigue and hyperpigmentation. Crisis is precipitated by stress or abrupt steroid withdrawal and treated with IV hydrocortisone, saline and dextrose. Clients are taught never to stop steroids abruptly and to double doses when ill.

Bottom line: Adrenal crisis presents with hyponatremia, hyperkalemia, hypoglycemia and hypotension, and is treated with IV hydrocortisone, normal saline and dextrose.

Sources: Ignatavicius, Med-Surg Nursing, 11th ed.; Lewis's Medical-Surgical Nursing, 13th ed.

Question 11 · Medical

A patient with severe hemophilia A is admitted after a fall with a large, expanding thigh hematoma and a hemoglobin drop from 13 to 9.4 g/dL. The aPTT is prolonged, and PT and platelets are normal. Which treatment is MOST specific?

  1. Tranexamic acid alone
  2. Factor VIII replacement
  3. Platelet transfusion
  4. Vitamin K 10 mg IV

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Antifibrinolytics are adjuncts for mucosal bleeding in hemophilia; a large expanding hematoma needs factor replacement.
  • B. Correct. Replacing the deficient factor is definitive therapy for bleeding in hemophilia A.
  • C. Platelet count and function are not the problem in hemophilia.
  • D. Vitamin K corrects deficiencies of factors II, VII, IX, and X, not factor VIII.

Hemophilia A (factor VIII deficiency) and hemophilia B (factor IX deficiency) cause an isolated prolonged aPTT with deep muscle and joint bleeding. Major bleeding is treated with prompt factor replacement to target levels (recombinant factor VIII, or bypassing agents or emicizumab-related protocols for patients with inhibitors), plus local measures. Nursing care avoids IM injections, NSAIDs, and arterial punctures, monitors for compartment syndrome, and coordinates with the hemophilia treatment center.

Sources: AACN Core Curriculum, 8th ed.; Goldman-Cecil Medicine, 27th ed.

Question 12 · Musculoskeletal and Wound

A 36-year-old was bitten on the palm by a neighbor's dog 4 hours ago. The wound has been irrigated and left open, and preemptive antibiotics are planned. She had anaphylaxis to amoxicillin as a teenager. Which regimen should the nurse anticipate?

  1. Trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole plus clindamycin
  2. Azithromycin alone for 5 days as a penicillin-free choice
  3. Metronidazole alone to cover the anaerobes of the dog's mouth
  4. Clindamycin alone, given at a higher than usual oral dose

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. For a penicillin-allergic patient with a dog bite, this pairing covers Pasteurella and the aerobic skin organisms along with the mouth anaerobes.
  • B. A macrolide avoids penicillin, which makes it tempting, but IDSA advises against macrolides for bites because of variable activity against Pasteurella multocida and fusobacteria.
  • C. Mouth anaerobes do take part in bite infections, but metronidazole acts on anaerobes and leaves Pasteurella, streptococci and staphylococci uncovered.
  • D. Clindamycin covers mouth anaerobes, streptococci and staphylococci, but it misses Pasteurella multocida, and a higher dose does not fix a gap in its spectrum.

Dog-bite infections are polymicrobial, mixing Pasteurella and other aerobes with mouth anaerobes, so the first-line agent is amoxicillin-clavulanate. When a patient cannot take penicillins, Merck lists trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole plus clindamycin, with doxycycline alone (not in children under 8 or in pregnancy) or, in adults, clindamycin plus a fluoroquinolone as other dog-bite options. Clindamycin alone is listed by IDSA as missing P. multocida, which is why it is paired, and macrolides are avoided. A palm wound qualifies for preemptive therapy for 3 to 5 days because it is a hand injury, and the nurse also confirms tetanus status and the dog's rabies vaccination and confinement.

Sources: Merck Manual Professional: Human and Mammal Bites (2025); IDSA Skin and Soft Tissue Infections (2014)

Question 13 · Head, Eye, Ear, Nose and Throat

A 12-year-old was hit in the right eye by a baseball an hour ago. Blood is layered in the lower quarter of the anterior chamber, the globe is intact, and ophthalmology will see the child this afternoon. Which instruction should the nurse give the family while they wait?

  1. Massage the closed lid gently to help the blood absorb
  2. Rest with the head of the bed raised to 45 degrees
  3. Give ibuprofen by weight for the pain
  4. Lie flat with both eyes patched to rest them

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Rubbing seems to speed clearing, but an injured eye is protected with a shield and nothing presses on it.
  • B. Correct. Head elevation lets the blood settle low in the anterior chamber, away from the visual axis and the drainage angle.
  • C. Ibuprofen is a common choice for pediatric pain, but NSAIDs and aspirin increase bleeding and the risk of a rebleed; acetaminophen is preferred.
  • D. Bilateral patching sounds restful, but lying flat lets the blood spread across the cornea; the injured eye is protected with a rigid shield instead.

A traumatic hyphema is blood in the anterior chamber, graded by how much of the chamber it fills. Early care is conservative: a rigid shield without pressure, limited activity, the head raised so blood settles inferiorly, and acetaminophen rather than aspirin or NSAIDs. Rebleeding usually happens within the first 5 days and tends to be worse than the first bleed, and the pressure inside the eye can rise, so the family returns at once for increasing pain, vomiting or worsening vision. In a young child, a large hyphema that blocks vision can also cause amblyopia.

Sources: AAO EyeWiki; ENA ENPC Provider Manual, 6th ed.; ENA TNCC Provider Manual, 9th ed.

Question 14 · Environment, Toxicology and Communicable Diseases

A client with severe burns to the anterior chest and neck has circumferential eschar and increasing difficulty breathing 8 hours after injury. Which intervention should the nurse anticipate?

  1. Tight pressure dressings over the chest wall
  2. Bronchodilators and humidified oxygen by mask
  3. Escharotomy to release the constricting eschar
  4. A diuretic to reduce chest wall edema

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Tight dressings worsen restriction.
  • B. The restriction is mechanical; drugs cannot expand a chest bound by eschar.
  • C. Correct. Circumferential chest burns restrict ventilation as eschar tightens; escharotomy relieves the constriction and allows chest expansion.
  • D. Diuretics worsen hypovolemia in burns.

Circumferential full-thickness burns of the chest restrict breathing; of the extremities they cause compartment syndrome. Escharotomy (incision through the eschar) is done at the bedside. Nursing care: monitoring respiratory effort, distal pulses and Doppler signals, and elevating burned extremities.

Bottom line: Circumferential full-thickness burns of the chest restrict breathing as eschar tightens and are treated with escharotomy; on extremities they cause compartment syndrome.

Sources: Ignatavicius, Med-Surg Nursing, 11th ed.; ABA ABLS Provider Manual (2023)

Question 15 · Professional Issues

An intoxicated 23-year-old with a split lip is spitting blood while a nurse holds pressure on his scalp laceration. He suddenly bites her forearm, breaking the skin, before security steps in and he is moved away from her. Which action should the nurse take FIRST?

  1. Dress the wound and finish the shift, since PEP can start up to 72 hours later
  2. Wash the bite wound with soap and water
  3. Draw his blood for HIV and hepatitis B testing from his existing IV line
  4. Call employee health before touching the wound, so the exposure time is recorded

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. CDC says HIV PEP must start within 72 hours, but sooner is better; waiting until the end of the shift delays both the medical evaluation and any prophylaxis.
  • B. Correct. NIOSH lists washing cuts and punctures with soap and water as the first step after a blood exposure, and a bite that breaks the skin while his mouth is bloody is a parenteral exposure.
  • C. Source testing is part of the follow-up, but OSHA requires the source patient's consent (or a documented legal exception) before his blood is tested, and it is not the first step.
  • D. Reporting is required and should happen promptly, but first aid is not delayed for the call; the wound is washed first and the time can be recorded afterward.

OSHA's bloodborne pathogens standard defines parenteral exposure as piercing the skin barrier through events such as needlesticks, human bites, cuts and abrasions, and any body fluid visibly contaminated with blood counts as potentially infectious, so this bite is an exposure incident. NIOSH lists the steps in order: wash punctures and cuts with soap and water, flush splashes, report the incident, and seek medical care at once. After the report, the employer must offer a confidential evaluation that documents the route, identifies the source patient, tests his blood with consent, tests the nurse and gives PEP when indicated. HIV PEP must begin within 72 hours, and earlier is better, which is why the report is not left to the end of the shift.

Sources: OSHA 29 CFR 1910.1030: Bloodborne Pathogens Standard; NIOSH: Bloodborne Infectious Disease Risk Factors (Healthcare Workers, 2025); CDC: HIV at Work (2026)
