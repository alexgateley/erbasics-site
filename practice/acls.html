Question 1 · Basic Life Support (BLS)

An adult who choked on meat is now unresponsive, and the nurse is performing CPR. After 30 compressions, the nurse opens the airway with a head tilt–chin lift and sees nothing in the mouth. What should the nurse do NEXT?

  1. Attempt 2 breaths without a finger sweep
  2. Sweep the back of the throat with a hooked finger to find the meat
  3. Give 5 abdominal thrusts before trying to ventilate
  4. Skip breaths and keep compressing until the meat comes into view

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. A visible object is removed when the airway is opened for breaths; when none is seen, breaths are attempted and blind sweeps are avoided.
  • B. Blind finger sweeps are classed as harmful in the 2025 guideline because they can push the object deeper and worsen the obstruction.
  • C. Abdominal thrusts are not used once the patient is unresponsive; compressions provide the pressure to move the object.
  • D. Compressions can dislodge the object, but breaths are still attempted; there is no evidence they worsen the obstruction.

During CPR for an unresponsive choking adult, the rescuer looks in the mouth each time the airway is opened for breaths and removes any object that can be seen, which helps prevent reobstruction. Observational data show the risk of a blind finger sweep outweighs any benefit, so the guideline lists it as a class 3 harm. Breaths are still attempted: there is no evidence they worsen the obstruction, and in theory they could move a complete obstruction into a mainstem bronchus and allow some ventilation, and compressions then continue.

Sources: AHA 2025 Part 7: Adult BLS

Question 2 · Rhythm Recognition

An ICU patient with a radial arterial line is in PEA arrest. During compressions, the arterial tracing shows only compression spikes. At the 2-minute rhythm check, the monitor shows sinus rhythm at 98/min, and the arterial line now shows a pulsatile waveform reading 94/56. Which action should come NEXT?

  1. Confirm a palpable pulse, then begin post-arrest care
  2. Give the scheduled epinephrine dose, then resume compressions
  3. Scan for cardiac motion with ultrasound before deciding
  4. Resume compressions until ETCO₂ rises above 40 mm Hg

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. A new arterial waveform during a check that shows an organized rhythm may indicate ROSC, and the guideline advises confirming that it matches a palpable pulse.
  • B. Arrest-dose epinephrine is for a patient without circulation. The arterial waveform suggests circulation has returned, which must be checked first.
  • C. Point-of-care ultrasound in arrest is not well established and tends to lengthen pauses; the arterial line already answers the question faster.
  • D. No absolute ETCO₂ cutoff for ROSC has been identified, and restarting compressions on a perfusing rhythm ignores the arterial data.

When an arterial line is in place, the 2025 guideline allows it to be used to detect ROSC, both during compressions and when a rhythm check shows an organized rhythm. The development of an arterial waveform or a sudden rise in diastolic pressure suggests ROSC, but the waveform should be correlated with a palpable pulse before stopping CPR. An abrupt rise in ETCO₂ is another clue, often more than 10 mm Hg, yet ROSC can occur with smaller rises. Once ROSC is confirmed, the team shifts to post-arrest care: oxygenation, ventilation, blood pressure targets and a 12-lead ECG.

Sources: AHA 2025 Part 9: Adult ALS

Question 3 · ACLS Pharmacology

A 69-year-old has been on an amiodarone infusion for 2 days after recurrent monomorphic VT. VT at 150 returns with BP 118/70, and the patient is alert. After expert consultation, procainamide is added to the amiodarone. Which dosing approach should the nurse expect?

  1. A 50 mg/min load, since amiodarone has already failed
  2. Stop amiodarone for 24 hours, then give the usual load
  3. A dose lower than usual, with close QRS and QTc checks
  4. The full 17 mg/kg load, since the two drugs act on different channels

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Amiodarone failure does not justify the fastest rate; a faster load raises the risk of hypotension and QRS widening, which the interaction makes more likely.
  • B. A 24-hour pause does nothing, because amiodarone's terminal half-life runs 9 to 36 days; the interaction persists, and stopping it removes VT protection.
  • C. Correct. Amiodarone raises procainamide and NAPA levels, so the label advises cutting the procainamide dose by about one-third and starting any added antiarrhythmic low.
  • D. Tempting because the two drugs belong to different classes, but the interaction is pharmacokinetic: amiodarone raises procainamide and NAPA concentrations whatever their channel effects.

The amiodarone injection label reports that given with procainamide for under 7 days, amiodarone raises plasma procainamide by 55% and NAPA by 33%, and it advises reducing the procainamide dose by one-third and starting any added antiarrhythmic at a lower than usual dose. Because amiodarone and its metabolite have terminal half-lives of weeks, the interaction continues after amiodarone stops. Both drugs can lengthen the QT and slow conduction, so the QRS, QTc and blood pressure are watched closely, and the procainamide endpoints (hypotension, QRS widening over 50%, suppression) still apply.

Sources: FDA Prescribing Information (DailyMed); AHA 2025 Adult ACLS Algorithms

Question 4 · ACLS Algorithms

Two hours after a temporary transvenous pacing wire is placed through the right internal jugular vein, a 74-year-old becomes hypotensive and then pulseless. The monitor shows paced complexes at 70/min, and CPR is started. The neck veins are distended, breath sounds are equal, and bedside ultrasound shows a large pericardial effusion with right ventricular diastolic collapse. Which intervention addresses the MOST likely cause?

  1. Epinephrine 1 mg IV and a 1-L fluid bolus
  2. Increase the pacing output to restore capture
  3. Emergency pericardiocentesis to drain the effusion
  4. Needle decompression of the right chest

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Epinephrine continues as part of the PEA algorithm and fluid may briefly support filling, but neither removes the blood compressing the heart.
  • B. Paced complexes without a pulse can look like a capture problem, but here the heart is being stimulated and cannot fill; more output does not relieve the compression.
  • C. Correct. A large effusion with right ventricular collapse in a patient who has just had a wire passed into the right ventricle points to tamponade, and draining the pericardium restores filling.
  • D. Tension pneumothorax also distends the neck veins after a jugular puncture, but breath sounds are equal and the ultrasound shows fluid around the heart.

Tamponade is one of the Ts. A pacing wire can perforate the thin right ventricle, and blood collecting in the pericardium compresses the heart, so electrical activity continues without output: here, paced complexes with no pulse. Distended neck veins with equal breath sounds and a large effusion with right ventricular diastolic collapse make tamponade the cause, while the equal breath sounds argue against tension pneumothorax. The treatment is to drain the pericardium by pericardiocentesis, ideally guided by ultrasound, or surgically, while CPR and the PEA algorithm continue. After recent cardiac surgery, emergency resternotomy takes the place of pericardiocentesis.

Sources: AHA 2025 Adult ACLS Algorithms; AHA 2025 Part 9: Adult ALS

Question 5 · Post-Cardiac Arrest Care

A man treated with temperature control at 36 °C is 96 hours from ROSC. Sedation has been off for 2 days, and electrolytes and glucose are normal. He does not yet follow commands. Data are: localizes to pain, NSE 14 mcg/L at 48 hours, continuous normal-voltage EEG on day 2, brisk pupillary and corneal reflexes. Which conclusion do these findings BEST support?

  1. The findings are unreliable because of the earlier temperature control
  2. Outcome is indeterminate until an MRI is done on day 7
  3. Poor outcome is likely because he does not follow commands at 72 hours
  4. A good neurologic outcome is likely, so full care continues

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. Tempting because cooling and sedation confound testing, but control at 36 °C has ended, sedation has been off for 2 days and the metabolic values are normal.
  • B. Tempting because MRI adds information, but an outcome is called indeterminate when neither good nor poor signs are present, and this patient already has several favorable ones.
  • C. Tempting because not obeying commands at 72 hours triggers formal prognostication, but that only makes him eligible for assessment; it is not a predictor of poor outcome.
  • D. Correct. A motor score of 5 at 72 to 96 hours, a normal NSE and a continuous normal-voltage EEG are each favorable signs.

Prognostication looks for signs of recovery as actively as for signs of poor outcome. The Singapore and Korean guidelines list a Glasgow motor score of 4 or 5 at 72 to 96 hours, a normal NSE (17 mcg/L or less) within 72 hours, a continuous normal-voltage EEG within 72 hours and no diffusion restriction on MRI at 2 to 7 days as markers of a likely good outcome. Localizing to pain is motor score 5, so with confounders cleared these concordant findings support continuing full treatment and rehabilitation planning.

Sources: Singapore Post-cardiac Arrest Care Guidelines (2026); 2025 Korean CPR Guidelines: Post-Cardiac Arrest Care; ERC/ESICM Post-Resuscitation Care Guidelines (2021)

Question 6 · Special Situations (LVAD/Peds)

An LVAD patient is found unresponsive, and the pump hum is faint. Which assessment findings indicate that perfusion is inadequate and chest compressions should begin?

  1. An absent radial pulse and no SpO₂ reading
  2. A Doppler MAP below 50 and ETCO₂ below 20
  3. Asystole on the monitor with audible pump hum
  4. A flat arterial waveform with a MAP of 72

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. These are common in well-perfused continuous-flow LVAD patients and do not define arrest.
  • B. Correct. These thresholds, along with unresponsiveness, indicate inadequate perfusion that warrants compressions.
  • C. LVAD patients can remain perfused in asystole or VF while the pump runs.
  • D. A MAP of 72 indicates adequate perfusion despite the flat waveform.

The AHA algorithm for LVAD patients who are unresponsive focuses on perfusion rather than pulses or rhythm. The team supports ventilation and assesses perfusion. Perfusion is adequate if any one of these is present: normal skin color and temperature, normal capillary refill, a MAP above 50 mm Hg (by Doppler or arterial line if the cuff fails), or an ETCO₂ above 20 mm Hg. An unresponsive patient with none of these, such as a Doppler MAP of 50 or less, an ETCO₂ of 20 or less, and cool, mottled skin, has impaired perfusion and gets compressions at once, while a second rescuer listens for the hum and checks alarms, the driveline, power, and the controller (AHA 2025, Part 10).

Sources: AACN Procedure Manual, 8th ed.; AHA 2025 Part 10: Special Circumstances

Question 7 · Basic Life Support (BLS)

A video review of a 10-minute ward resuscitation with 30:2 bag-mask CPR shows that after each set of 30 compressions the nurse at the head takes about 12 to 15 seconds to reseal the mask and deliver 2 breaths. Data are: compression rate 108/min, depth 5.2 cm, full recoil, chest compression fraction 55%. While keeping 30:2 CPR, which change is MOST likely to raise the compression fraction?

  1. Swap compressors after each set of 30 to keep the depth on target
  2. Pause each cycle until both breaths visibly raise the chest
  3. Keep each breath pause under 10 seconds, then resume compressions
  4. Have the compressor speed up to 120/min to make up for the pauses

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Each swap adds a hand-off pause, and the depth is already 5.2 cm; compressors change at the 2-minute rhythm check, not every cycle.
  • B. Effective breaths matter, but the open-ended pause is what is costing compression time; the fix is a better seal and head position within a pause under 10 seconds, not a longer stop.
  • C. Correct. Two breaths should take less than 10 seconds; if a breath fails, compressions resume and the airway is improved before the next pair.
  • D. A faster rate shortens each set of 30 from about 17 to 15 seconds while the breath pause stays the same, so the fraction falls slightly; 108/min is already in range.

Chest compression fraction is the share of time spent compressing, and the Korean 2025 guideline asks for at least 60%, kept as high as possible, because blood flow and coronary perfusion fall within seconds of each pause. Here 30 compressions take about 17 seconds at 108/min, so a 13-second breath pause after each set holds the fraction near 55% even though rate, depth and recoil are on target. MedlinePlus sets the limit for the pause: breaths should interrupt compressions for less than 10 seconds, and a 9-second pause would lift the fraction to about 65%. A two-handed mask seal, a good head position and breaths of about 1 second each keep it short.

Sources: Korean CPR Guidelines 2025 Part 3: Adult BLS; MedlinePlus: CPR - Adult and Child After Puberty (2026); AHA 2025 Part 7: Adult BLS

Question 8 · Rhythm Recognition

A 79-year-old with severe aortic stenosis awaiting valve replacement develops new atrial fibrillation at 118/min. Within 20 minutes her BP falls from 136/72 to 78/46, she becomes confused, and crackles rise to the mid-lung fields. Data are: SpO₂ 88% on 4 L, potassium 4.4 mEq/L, no preexcitation on the 12-lead ECG. Which treatment is MOST appropriate?

  1. Furosemide 40 mg IV for the pulmonary edema
  2. Synchronized cardioversion, sedated if feasible
  3. Amiodarone 150 mg IV over 10 minutes
  4. IV diltiazem 0.25 mg/kg over 2 minutes for rate control

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. The congestion comes from lost atrial filling, and diuresis lowers preload in a ventricle that depends on it, so it risks deeper hypotension while the cause continues.
  • B. Correct. Her collapse began with the AF, and a ventricle stiffened by aortic stenosis depends on atrial contraction, so instability attributable to AF calls for immediate synchronized cardioversion.
  • C. Amiodarone can slow or convert AF, but conversion may take hours, it can lower blood pressure, and immediate electrical cardioversion is recommended when instability is due to AF.
  • D. A slower rate would lengthen filling, but diltiazem's vasodilation and negative inotropy can deepen hypotension behind a fixed outflow obstruction, and it does not restore the lost atrial contraction.

Rates under 150/min are less often the cause of instability, so the team first asks whether the AF explains the collapse. Here it does: the pressure fell as soon as AF began, and severe aortic stenosis leaves a thick, stiff ventricle that relies on atrial contraction, which normally adds about 15 to 30% of stroke volume and more in this lesion. The 2023 AF guideline gives immediate electrical cardioversion a Class 1 recommendation for hemodynamic instability attributable to AF, with the shock confirmed as synchronized to the QRS. The 2025 algorithm starts AF at 200 J or more, raised if a shock fails, and sedation is given when it will not delay treatment.

Sources: ACC/AHA/ACCP/HRS AF Guideline (2023); AHA 2025 Adult ACLS Algorithms; Merck Manual Professional: Atrial Fibrillation (2024)

Question 9 · ACLS Pharmacology

A 74-year-old receiving IV erythromycin has recurrent runs of torsades de pointes, each starting after a long pause that follows a premature beat. Erythromycin has been stopped and IV magnesium sulfate was given 20 minutes ago, but the runs continue. Data are: sinus bradycardia 46 between runs, BP 108/66, QTc 610 ms, K+ 4.4 mEq/L. A cardiologist has been called. Which therapy should the nurse anticipate NEXT?

  1. Amiodarone 150 mg IV over 10 minutes
  2. An esmolol bolus followed by a titrated infusion
  3. Procainamide 20 to 50 mg/min IV until suppressed
  4. Overdrive pacing or an isoproterenol infusion

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. Tempting because it is the usual drug for VT with a pulse, but the AHA warns that antiarrhythmics given for torsades can lengthen the QT further and promote the arrhythmia.
  • B. Beta-blockers help in ischemic polymorphic VT, but the AHA notes that given acutely they can precipitate torsades by causing or worsening bradycardia, the very trigger here.
  • C. Procainamide is a class IA drug; its label lists torsades de pointes as a contraindication to class IA agents, and the 2025 algorithm says to avoid it with a prolonged QT.
  • D. Correct. When torsades is precipitated by bradycardia or pauses and continues after magnesium, the 2025 AHA guideline advises expert consultation for overdrive pacing or isoproterenol; a faster rate removes the pauses that set off each run.

Torsades de pointes typically recurs as self-terminating runs of polymorphic VT in a patient with a long QT, often with an associated bradycardia. IV magnesium suppresses recurrences in case series, and offending drugs are stopped and hypokalemia corrected. When runs continue and are precipitated by bradycardia or pauses, the 2025 AHA guideline advises expert consultation for overdrive pacing or isoproterenol. Antiarrhythmics that lengthen the QT and acutely given beta-blockers can worsen torsades, and a sustained run is treated with an immediate unsynchronized shock.

Sources: AHA 2025 Part 9: Adult ALS; AHA 2025 Adult ACLS Algorithms; FDA Prescribing Information (DailyMed)

Question 10 · ACLS Algorithms

A 66-year-old with metformin-associated lactic acidosis was breathing 34/min before intubation for exhaustion; his ABG then was pH 7.11, PaCO₂ 16 mm Hg, HCO₃⁻ 5 mEq/L. Twenty minutes after intubation on assist-control at a rate of 14 and a tidal volume of 450 mL, he becomes hypotensive and bradycardic. Repeat ABG: pH 6.72, PaCO₂ 41 mm Hg. Which change BEST addresses the cause?

  1. Start norepinephrine and recheck the gas in 1 hour
  2. Raise the set rate to match his breathing before intubation
  3. Lower the rate to 10/min to improve venous return
  4. Give sodium bicarbonate 150 mEq IV and keep the settings

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. A vasopressor may be needed, but it does not treat the rising CO₂ behind the fall in pH, and waiting an hour at a pH of 6.72 risks cardiac arrest.
  • B. Correct. Before intubation he was blowing off CO₂ to offset the acid load; 14 breaths of 450 mL is about 6 L/min, far below his earlier minute ventilation, so CO₂ rose and the pH fell to a dangerous level. Matching his previous ventilation, while watching for auto-PEEP, removes the cause.
  • C. Fewer breaths lower intrathoracic pressure, which can help hypotension after intubation, but here they would raise the CO₂ further and deepen the acidemia that is driving the collapse.
  • D. Bicarbonate may be considered at a pH under 7.0, but part of it is converted to CO₂, which he cannot clear at the current settings, so the acidemia could worsen; the ventilation problem comes first.

Hydrogen ion excess is one of the Hs. In severe metabolic acidosis, the patient compensates by breathing fast and deep, and the low PaCO₂ keeps the pH from falling further. Sedation, paralysis and a standard ventilator rate remove that compensation, so the PaCO₂ climbs toward normal and the pH plunges, which depresses the heart and can lead to bradycardia, hypotension and arrest. Ventilator settings are adjusted to the higher minute ventilation the patient needs, with an arterial gas soon after intubation. Bicarbonate is considered for very severe acidemia only with care, because it generates CO₂ that must be exhaled.

Sources: Merck Manual Professional: Acid-Base Disorders (2025); AHA ACLS Provider Manual (2025)

Question 11 · Post-Cardiac Arrest Care

Paramedics achieve ROSC in a man after a witnessed VF arrest and place an endotracheal tube. His hands are cold, and the pulse oximeter shows an intermittent reading of 94% without a consistent waveform. Which oxygen strategy is MOST appropriate during transport?

  1. Set the FiO₂ at 0.6 as a middle ground
  2. Lower the FiO₂ to 0.4 now to limit hyperoxic reperfusion injury
  3. Titrate FiO₂ to the intermittent 94% reading
  4. Give 100% oxygen until SpO₂ can be measured reliably

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. Tempting as a compromise, but no fixed intermediate FiO₂ is recommended; full oxygen continues until a reliable SpO₂ or PaO₂ can guide titration.
  • B. Tempting because hyperoxemia may worsen oxidative injury, but lowering FiO₂ before saturation can be measured risks unrecognized hypoxemia, which the EXACT trial linked to lower survival.
  • C. Tempting because 94% sits inside the 90% to 98% target, but a reading without a consistent waveform during poor perfusion is not reliable enough to titrate against.
  • D. Correct. Full oxygen is recommended after ROSC until SpO₂ or PaO₂ can be measured reliably, which avoids unrecognized hypoxemia.

The 2025 AHA guideline recommends 100% inspired oxygen after ROSC until SpO₂ or PaO₂ can be measured reliably (class 1), and hypoxemia should be avoided. Poor perfusion and prehospital equipment often make early oximetry unreliable. The EXACT randomized trial found that adjusting FiO₂ to a lower SpO₂ target in the prehospital setting produced more hypoxemic episodes, which is why titration down to an SpO₂ of 90% to 98% waits until a trustworthy reading or an arterial blood gas is available.

Sources: AHA 2025 Part 11: Post-Cardiac Arrest Care; AHA 2025 Adult ACLS Algorithms

Question 12 · Special Situations (LVAD/Peds)

A patient took several extra buprenorphine films and is now somnolent. Data are: RR 6, SpO₂ 83%, pulse 76, BP 128/80. After naloxone 0.4 mg and then 2 mg IV, the RR is 8 and shallow, and bag-mask ventilation continues. A colleague points out that buprenorphine has a ceiling effect on breathing. What should the team do NEXT?

  1. Keep ventilating and give larger repeat naloxone doses
  2. Give naloxone 0.04 mg to avoid precipitating withdrawal
  3. Stop naloxone because the reversal will stay incomplete
  4. Stop assisting, since the ceiling effect caps depression

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. Reversal of buprenorphine may be incomplete or need higher or repeated naloxone doses, and breathing is assisted while the response is partial.
  • B. Tempting because he is likely opioid dependent, but tiny doses suit partial postoperative reversal; buprenorphine needs larger doses, not smaller ones.
  • C. Tempting because buprenorphine binds tightly and dissociates slowly, but large naloxone doses do antagonize it, with a gradual onset of reversal.
  • D. Tempting because buprenorphine's ceiling effect is real, but this patient is hypoxemic at RR 8, so the ceiling has not protected him and ventilation must continue.

Buprenorphine is a partial mu agonist that binds slowly and dissociates slowly, so standard naloxone doses may only partly reverse its respiratory depression. The naloxone label states that large doses are required to antagonize buprenorphine, that the reversal has a gradual onset, and that respirations should be mechanically assisted when the response is incomplete. Because buprenorphine also has a long duration of action, the patient needs prolonged monitoring for recurrent respiratory depression after he improves.

Sources: FDA Prescribing Information (DailyMed); Merck Manual Professional: Opioid Toxicity and Withdrawal (2026); AHA 2025 Part 10: Special Circumstances

Question 13 · Basic Life Support (BLS)

Lifeguards pull an unresponsive adult from a hotel pool. He is not breathing and has no pulse. A trained nurse arrives with an AED at the same moment the lifeguards are ready to start resuscitation. What should the rescuers do FIRST?

  1. Apply a nonrebreather mask at 15 L/min while the AED powers on
  2. Start CPR with rescue breaths before attaching the AED
  3. Attach the AED and analyze before compressions
  4. Give compressions without breaths until the pads are on

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. A mask delivers oxygen only to a patient who is breathing; a pulseless, apneic patient needs ventilations and compressions.
  • B. Correct. Drowning causes a hypoxic arrest, and the 2025 guideline recommends starting CPR with breaths and compressions before an AED or defibrillator is applied.
  • C. Early defibrillation drives survival in sudden cardiac arrest from VF, but a drowning arrest is asphyxial and usually nonshockable, so oxygen delivery comes first.
  • D. Compression-only CPR is reasonable after drowning only when a rescuer is unwilling, untrained, or unable to give breaths; these rescuers are trained.

In drowning, the arrest results from hypoxia, so restoring oxygenation is the priority. The 2025 AHA special circumstances guideline gives a class 1 recommendation that CPR with breaths and compressions be started before AED application, while still calling AED use reasonable once CPR is under way. Trained rescuers ventilate by the first means available and add supplemental oxygen when they can. This differs from witnessed sudden collapse, where VF is more likely and the AED goes on as soon as it arrives.

Sources: AHA 2025 Part 10: Special Circumstances; AHA 2025 Part 7: Adult BLS

Question 14 · Rhythm Recognition

The telemetry technician calls a VF alarm for a 74-year-old on day 2 after hip surgery. At the central station, leads II and V1 show chaotic, irregular waves. Lead III shows narrow QRS complexes at 84/min, and the SpO₂ waveform is regular at 84/min. Which interpretation is MOST likely?

  1. VF, with the oximeter counting the electrical noise as a pulse
  2. Motion artifact that mimics VF in two of the leads
  3. Polymorphic VT seen in leads II and V1 but missed by lead III
  4. VF, with a loose lead III electrode showing an older rhythm

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Tempting because oximeters can be fooled, but a regular plethysmographic waveform at the same rate as the clean lead's QRS complexes points to real perfusion, which VF cannot produce.
  • B. Correct. True VF is the heart's own electrical activity and shows in every lead, whereas artifact from movement such as tooth brushing or shivering can contaminate some leads and spare one, while the oximeter keeps counting a regular pulse.
  • C. Polymorphic VT is a ventricular rhythm, so it would distort the QRS in lead III too; a lead showing narrow complexes at a steady rate argues against any ventricular tachyarrhythmia.
  • D. A loose electrode produces noise or a lead-fail alert in the leads that use it, not a clean narrow-complex trace matching the pulse rate. A faulty right-arm or chest electrode would instead explain the noise in leads II and V1, which lead III does not use.

In a 31-day study of 461 ICU patients, Drew and colleagues found that about 89% of annotated arrhythmia alarms were false, and artifact that mimicked VF sometimes spared just one lead, which was often not the lead on display. Motion during daily activities such as brushing teeth produces rapid, repetitive artifact. Because VF arises in the ventricles, it appears in every lead and leaves no pulse, so a clean lead and a regular pleth at the same rate identify artifact. The nurse still goes to the bedside at once to confirm the patient is responsive, and keeps arrhythmia detection on rather than suspending it.

Sources: AACN Practice Alert: Managing Alarms (2018)

Question 15 · ACLS Pharmacology

A patient with symptomatic bradycardia does not respond to atropine, and a dopamine infusion is started while transcutaneous pacing is prepared. What is the usual dopamine infusion range for this indication?

  1. 0.5–1 mcg/kg/min
  2. 5–20 mcg/kg/min
  3. 0.01–0.04 units/min
  4. 40–180 mcg/min

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. This range is too low for a meaningful chronotropic effect.
  • B. Correct. The usual dopamine range for bradycardia is 5–20 mcg/kg/min, titrated to response.
  • C. This is the dosing unit for vasopressin.
  • D. This is a typical phenylephrine range, not dopamine.

For symptomatic bradycardia unresponsive to atropine, the algorithm lists dopamine at a usual infusion rate of 5–20 mcg/kg/min, titrated to response and tapered slowly, or epinephrine at 2–10 mcg/min, as alternatives or bridges to pacing. Both increase myocardial oxygen demand and arrhythmia risk. Transvenous pacing is arranged for persistent high-grade block.

Sources: AHA 2025 Adult ACLS Algorithms; AHA 2025 Part 9: Adult ALS; AHA ACLS Provider Manual (2025)
