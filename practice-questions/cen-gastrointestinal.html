Question 1

A 54-year-old kidney transplant recipient who takes prednisone, tacrolimus and mycophenolate has had 2 days of abdominal pain and poor appetite. In the waiting room she says the pain is "not that bad." Data are: T 37.5°C (99.5°F), HR 106/min, BP 112/70 mm Hg, WBC 9,200/mm³. Her abdomen has mild diffuse tenderness without guarding. Which statement BEST guides the nurse's assessment?

  1. The absence of guarding lowers the concern for perforation or peritonitis
  2. Her medicines can mute the pain, fever and white cell response to perforation or peritonitis
  3. A normal white cell count makes an intra-abdominal infection unlikely
  4. Gastrointestinal side effects of mycophenolate and tacrolimus explain her symptoms

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Guarding reflects peritoneal inflammation in a person with a normal immune response. In a patient taking steroids, its absence is less reassuring, and the tachycardia here is a warning.
  • B. Correct. Merck notes that prednisone and other immunosuppressants can inhibit the inflammatory response to perforation or peritonitis, so tenderness and leukocytosis may be less than expected.
  • C. A white count is often used to screen for infection, but immunosuppressants can keep it normal despite perforation or abscess, so it does not exclude a surgical cause in this patient.
  • D. Both drugs commonly cause nausea, diarrhea or abdominal discomfort, which makes this tempting, but attributing 2 days of pain with tachycardia to side effects before excluding a surgical cause risks a missed perforation.

Merck singles out patients taking prednisone or other immunosuppressants as a group in whom abdominal pain is of particular concern, because these drugs blunt the inflammatory response: pain, tenderness, fever and leukocytosis can all be less than the disease would normally produce. Appendicitis, diverticulitis or a perforated viscus can therefore look mild. The tachycardia is the most objective warning here. The nurse should not let her own description of the pain lower her priority, should tell the provider about her transplant medicines, anticipate early imaging, and reassess vital signs often while she waits.

Sources: Merck Manual Professional: Acute Abdominal Pain (2024); Merck Manual Professional: Appendicitis (2024)

Question 2

A 26-year-old with a single stab wound to the left upper anterior abdomen is being managed with serial examinations after arriving hemodynamically stable without peritoneal signs. Four hours later the nurse reassesses him. Which finding should the nurse report to the surgeon at once?

  1. Oozing from the wound that stops after 5 minutes of direct pressure
  2. Hemoglobin 13.8 g/dL, down from 14.4 g/dL after 1 L of saline
  3. Tenderness at the wound edges, rated 5/10, unchanged since arrival
  4. New guarding and rebound tenderness spreading across the abdomen, away from the wound

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. External oozing that stops with pressure is a minor wound problem. It does not indicate intra-abdominal injury and is managed with a dressing.
  • B. A small fall after a liter of crystalloid is expected from dilution, which is why it worries some nurses. A larger or continuing fall, or one with tachycardia, would be reported.
  • C. Local pain at a stab wound is expected and unchanged pain is reassuring. It is the spread of tenderness beyond the wound with guarding that signals peritonitis.
  • D. Correct. Spreading guarding and rebound are peritoneal signs, which indicate injury to bowel or another intra-abdominal organ. Merck lists peritonitis as an indication for laparotomy without further testing.

Merck describes selective management of anterior abdominal stab wounds: a stable patient without peritoneal signs may have local wound exploration and, if the fascia is penetrated, admission for serial examinations; observation generally means repeated examinations, preferably by the same examiner, with a CBC every 4 to 6 hours. Laparotomy is performed if peritoneal signs or hemodynamic instability develop, and evisceration and peritonitis are indications to operate without more testing. FAST is poor at detecting a perforated viscus, so the abdominal examination over time is the main safeguard. The nurse documents each examination in the same terms, keeps the patient NPO, and reports new peritoneal signs, rising heart rate or falling pressure immediately.

Sources: Merck Manual Professional: Overview of Abdominal Trauma (2025); ENA TNCC Provider Manual, 9th ed.

Question 3

A 4-year-old swallowed several small, high-powered magnets from a desk toy about 3 hours ago. He is playing, has eaten crackers without trouble, and his abdomen is soft and nontender. Data are: HR 104/min, RR 22/min, T 37.0°C (98.6°F). Abdominal films show a cluster of round metallic densities in the left upper quadrant, consistent with the stomach. His mother asks whether she can take him home and check his stools. Which plan should the nurse anticipate?

  1. Admit for serial abdominal exams and call surgery if pain develops
  2. Keep him NPO and prepare for urgent endoscopic removal from the stomach
  3. Polyethylene glycol to speed the magnets through, with a film at 6-hour intervals
  4. Discharge with stool checks for 2 weeks, since he has no pain or vomiting

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Observation is reasonable for objects with a low complication rate, but pain from attracted magnets often appears only after the bowel wall has already been injured, so waiting for symptoms defeats the purpose.
  • B. Correct. Multiple magnets are among the ingestions that must be removed, and the adult ESGE guideline places magnets in the stomach in the urgent (within 24 hours) endoscopy group. They are within reach now, so removing them prevents bowel injury that can develop silently.
  • C. Speeding transit sounds safer than waiting, but it moves the magnets out of endoscopic reach into the small bowel, where they can trap a fold of bowel wall between them. While they are still in the stomach, retrieval is the safer step.
  • D. Stool monitoring is the usual plan for a single coin, and a single magnet is unlikely to cause harm, which makes this tempting in a well child. Several magnets can attract each other across loops of bowel and injure the wall while the child still looks well.

Single swallowed magnets usually pass, but the Merck Manual warns that multiple magnets are extremely hazardous because they can attract each other across loops of bowel. The trapped wall is squeezed between them and can necrose and perforate, often in a child who looked well at first. Au and Goldman list multiple magnets with button batteries as objects that must be removed, and the adult ESGE guideline groups magnets in the stomach with batteries and sharp objects for urgent endoscopy within 24 hours. Because these magnets are still in the stomach, the window for a simple endoscopic retrieval is open now, so the nurse keeps the child NPO, prepares for the procedure and explains to the mother why a well appearance is not reassuring here.

Sources: Au & Goldman, Gastric Metallic Foreign Bodies in Children (2021); ESGE Guideline: Removal of Upper GI Foreign Bodies in Adults (2016)

Question 4

A 74-year-old walks into triage 1 hour after swallowing one of her hearing aid batteries, which she mistook for a pill. She has no pain, cough, drooling or trouble swallowing, and the pack holds no magnets. Her husband says he will check her stools and bring her back if anything changes. Which part of her history means she needs a radiograph now?

  1. Arrival about 1 hour after she swallowed it
  2. Apixaban taken daily for atrial fibrillation
  3. Two past dilations of an esophageal stricture
  4. A size 312 hearing aid cell, about 8 mm across

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Early arrival might seem to call for a film before damage starts, but the time since ingestion is not one of the imaging criteria for a small battery in a patient older than 12.
  • B. Anticoagulation raises concern about bleeding from any injury, which makes it tempting, but it is not one of the criteria that decide whether an x-ray is needed after a small battery ingestion.
  • C. Correct. Prior esophageal stricture, surgery or motility disease removes the option of skipping imaging, because a narrowed lumen is where a battery can lodge and burn.
  • D. A battery is alarming, but hearing aid batteries are assumed to be under 12 mm, and a single small battery in an adult without symptoms can be managed without imaging when the other criteria are met.

For patients older than 12 who swallow one battery of 12 mm or less, the National Capital Poison Center allows home observation without an x-ray only when every condition holds: no symptoms since ingestion, a single battery, no magnet, a size reliably known (hearing aid cells are assumed smaller than 12 mm), no history of esophageal surgery, stricture, motility disorder or other esophageal disease, and a reliable patient or caregiver. Her stricture history fails that test, so she needs immediate films of the neck, esophagus and abdomen, since an esophageal battery can cause serious burns within 2 hours.

Sources: National Capital Poison Center Button Battery Guideline

Question 5

A 63-year-old with type 2 diabetes and known reflux arrives at triage 40 minutes after dinner with epigastric burning, nausea and fatigue. He asks for an antacid, saying "it's just my reflux, but worse." Data are: BP 142/86 mm Hg, HR 84/min, RR 18/min, SpO₂ 97%. He has no chest pain. Which action should the triage nurse take FIRST?

  1. Give the oral antacid he requests and reassess the pain in 30 minutes
  2. Draw a lipase and liver panel, then return him to the waiting room
  3. Place him in fast track and request an order for a GI cocktail
  4. Obtain a 12-lead ECG within 10 minutes of arrival, before any GI workup

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. Treating the complaint is patient-centered, but medication without an order, and a 30-minute wait before evaluation, delays recognition of an infarction that can present exactly this way.
  • B. Pancreatitis and biliary disease are on the list for epigastric pain, but labs can follow; an ECG takes minutes and the one diagnosis that cannot wait is a STEMI.
  • C. His history and stable vitals make a reflux flare plausible, but relief from an antacid mixture does not exclude ischemia, and fast track delays the test that would show an infarction.
  • D. Correct. Epigastric burning with nausea can be how ischemia presents, especially with diabetes, and suspected ACS calls for an ECG within 10 minutes of presentation to medical care.

Atypical ACS presentations such as epigastric pain, isolated dyspnea, nausea or fatigue without chest pain are more common in women, older adults and people with diabetes, and some patients describe ischemic discomfort as indigestion. People with diabetes can also have silent ischemia, so the absence of chest pain does not reassure. The Merck Manual and StatPearls, reflecting the 2025 ACC/AHA guideline, state that a 12-lead ECG should be obtained within 10 minutes of presentation in anyone with suspected ACS, because ST elevation triggers immediate reperfusion; inferior-lead ST elevation can be subtle. A normal first ECG does not end the workup: serial ECGs and high-sensitivity troponin follow. Gastrointestinal causes such as reflux, peptic ulcer and pancreatitis are considered after the ECG has been read.

Sources: Merck Manual, Professional Version; StatPearls (NCBI Bookshelf); ACC/AHA ACS Guideline (2025)

Question 6

A 31-year-old in the ED has CT-confirmed uncomplicated appendicitis with an appendicolith. Data are: T 37.9°C (100.2°F), HR 92/min, BP 128/78 mm Hg, WBC 13,200/mm³. While waiting for the surgeon, he says a coworker was treated with antibiotics alone and asks to do the same so he can avoid an operation. Which response by the nurse is MOST accurate?

  1. The stone seen on your scan makes antibiotics alone more likely to fail
  2. Antibiotics alone are offered mainly to pregnant patients, to avoid anesthesia
  3. Antibiotics alone suit you, because the scan shows no perforation or abscess
  4. Antibiotics alone can be tried after 24 hours of observation without antibiotics

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. WSES 2020 limits antibiotic-only care to selected uncomplicated cases without an appendicolith, and Merck still recommends appendectomy, especially when CT shows one. The surgeon should discuss this so his choice is informed.
  • B. This reverses the guidance. WSES 2020 suggests against nonoperative management of appendicitis in pregnancy until better evidence exists; antibiotic-only care is studied mainly in nonpregnant adults and children.
  • C. Uncomplicated disease is the first requirement, which makes this tempting, but it is not the only one: the appendicolith raises the failure rate and moves him out of the group WSES considers suitable.
  • D. Watching without treatment is not the nonoperative pathway. WSES describes starting with IV antibiotics and switching to oral ones as the patient improves; untreated observation risks progression to gangrene and perforation.

The WSES 2020 Jerusalem guideline calls antibiotic therapy a safe alternative to surgery only for selected patients with uncomplicated appendicitis and no appendicolith, and it asks that patients be told about the chance of failure and of missing a complicated appendicitis. An appendicolith predicts failure, which is why WSES recommends surgery for children who have one. Merck adds that many patients treated with antibiotics recover during the first admission, but a significant number recur and need appendectomy within the year. The nurse gives accurate information and makes sure the surgeon has the consent conversation; the patient still decides.

Sources: WSES Jerusalem Guidelines: Acute Appendicitis (2020 update); Merck Manual Professional: Appendicitis (2024)

Question 7

A 60-year-old has had a painful right groin bulge for 8 hours that he could usually push back in but now cannot. The bulge is tense and tender, the skin over it is red, and he has vomited twice. Data are: T 38.3°C (100.9°F), HR 112, BP 128/80 mm Hg, WBC 15,600/mm³. The provider is busy with a trauma patient, and a colleague offers to try to reduce the hernia. Which action should the nurse take?

  1. Keep him NPO, start IV access, and request urgent surgical evaluation
  2. Place him in Trendelenburg and apply gentle, steady pressure to reduce it
  3. Apply ice and a scrotal support, then reassess in 1 hour
  4. Give the ordered analgesic, then let the colleague attempt reduction

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. Fever, erythema, tenderness, tachycardia and leukocytosis suggest a strangulated hernia with compromised blood supply, a surgical emergency; reducing it is not attempted.
  • B. This technique can reduce an uncomplicated incarcerated hernia, but here the signs point to strangulation, and pushing the loop back can return ischemic bowel to the abdomen before anyone can check its viability.
  • C. Comfort measures alone delay surgery; strangulated bowel can become gangrenous within hours, and the clock started at least 8 hours ago.
  • D. Analgesia is reasonable, but reduction is a provider decision, and a probable strangulation needs urgent surgery, not an attempt to push the bowel back.

An incarcerated hernia cannot be reduced but keeps its blood supply; a strangulated hernia is irreducible with compromised blood supply and is a surgical emergency. Merck describes strangulation as steady, worsening pain with nausea and vomiting, a tender hernia and red overlying skin, and here fever and leukocytosis add evidence of ischemia. Merck states that any incarcerated or strangulated hernia needs urgent surgical repair. HerniaSurge notes the concern that a strangulated hernia reduced before surgery hides bowel whose viability has not been assessed, which is why surgeons may explore the abdomen after a spontaneous reduction. The nurse keeps him NPO, starts IV access and fluids, gives analgesia and escalates to the surgeon.

Sources: Merck Manual Professional: Inguinal Hernia (2024); StatPearls: Open Inguinal Hernia Repair (2026); HerniaSurge International Groin Hernia Guidelines (2018)

Question 8

A 31-year-old at 22 weeks' gestation has ultrasound-confirmed acute calculous cholecystitis. She is receiving IV fluids, antibiotics and analgesia, and fetal heart tones are 148/min. She asks whether the operation can wait until after the baby is born. Which response by the nurse is MOST accurate?

  1. Early laparoscopic removal of the gallbladder is generally favored in pregnancy
  2. A drain placed through the skin into the gallbladder is the usual choice in pregnancy
  3. An ERCP to clear the bile duct can treat the gallbladder without surgery
  4. Antibiotics can hold the infection until after delivery, when surgery is safer

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. Merck lists pregnancy among the situations in which early cholecystectomy is generally favored, and the operation is usually done laparoscopically.
  • B. Percutaneous cholecystostomy is an alternative for patients at very high surgical risk, such as critically ill or frail older adults, not the routine choice for a healthy pregnant woman.
  • C. ERCP removes stones from the common bile duct, which is useful when the duct is obstructed, but it does not remove an inflamed gallbladder blocked at the cystic duct.
  • D. Delaying surgery sounds protective of the pregnancy, but antibiotics alone are not definitive treatment, their benefit is unproven in mild cholecystitis, and an untreated inflamed gallbladder can perforate, which threatens both mother and fetus.

Merck recommends cholecystectomy for acute cholecystitis within 7 days of hospital admission and within 10 days of the onset of symptoms, and notes that early surgery is generally favored in patients older than 80, pregnant patients and patients with cirrhosis. The operation is usually laparoscopic. Antibiotics are usually started, but evidence of benefit is lacking in mild cases, and without definitive treatment about 10% of patients perforate, with mortality up to 16%. Percutaneous cholecystostomy is reserved for patients at very high surgical risk. The nurse explains why the team favors early surgery, involves the obstetric team for fetal monitoring around the procedure, and positions her off her back to keep the uterus from compressing the vena cava.

Sources: Merck Manual Professional: Acute Cholecystitis (2025)

Question 9

A 13-month-old has had 30 hours of screaming episodes with drawn-up legs and now has green vomit and a currant-jelly stool. He is lethargic, and his abdomen is distended, rigid and tender to light touch. Data are: T 38.7°C (101.7°F), HR 186/min, capillary refill 4 seconds. Ultrasound shows a target sign. Radiology calls to say the room is ready for an air enema. Which plan should the nurse anticipate?

  1. Fluid resuscitation, antibiotics and gastric suction, then surgery
  2. Air enema now, since it reduces most ileocolic cases without surgery
  3. A barium enema, which can confirm the diagnosis and reduce it
  4. CT with IV contrast to look for a lead point such as a polyp before treatment

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. Merck directs ill children with peritonitis to fluid resuscitation, broad-spectrum antibiotics, nasogastric suction and surgery rather than enema reduction.
  • B. Air enema succeeds in over 80% of children and is the usual treatment, which makes it tempting, but a rigid, tender abdomen suggests perforation, and pressurizing perforated bowel is dangerous.
  • C. Barium enema was once used to diagnose and reduce intussusception, but barium can leak into the peritoneum through a perforation, and this child already shows peritoneal signs.
  • D. Lead points such as Meckel diverticulum or lymphoma do occur, but a CT delays care for a child in shock with peritonitis, and the ultrasound has already made the diagnosis.

Intussusception most often affects children between 6 months and 3 years. Colicky pain every 15 to 20 minutes with vomiting gives way to steady pain, lethargy and currant-jelly stool, which Merck calls a late sign. Ultrasound with a target sign is the preferred study, and an air enema reduces most ileocolic cases in stable children. Tenderness, guarding and rigidity suggest perforation, and pallor, tachycardia and delayed refill suggest shock; these children get fluids, broad-spectrum antibiotics and nasogastric decompression and go to surgery. Survival and the chance of nonoperative reduction fall with time, so delays matter.

Sources: Merck Manual Professional: Intussusception (2025)

Question 10

A 76-year-old is brought from home with 2 days of right upper quadrant pain, yellow eyes and shaking chills, and in the ED he becomes confused. Data are: T 39.4°C (102.9°F), HR 118/min, BP 84/50 mm Hg, total bilirubin 5.8 mg/dL, alkaline phosphatase 410 U/L, WBC 19,600/mm³. Bedside ultrasound shows gallstones and a common bile duct of 11 mm with the gallbladder in place. Blood cultures, piperacillin-tazobactam and fluid boluses have been started. Which intervention treats the cause of his illness?

  1. Percutaneous cholecystostomy tube
  2. Urgent ERCP to clear and drain the common bile duct
  3. Cholescintigraphy to confirm cystic duct blockage
  4. Emergent laparoscopic cholecystectomy

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. A cholecystostomy is the alternative for very high-risk patients with acute cholecystitis, and his age and shock make it tempting, but it drains the gallbladder, not the obstructed common duct that is causing the sepsis.
  • B. Correct. Acute cholangitis is an emergency that needs urgent stone removal and drainage, and ERCP with sphincterotomy succeeds in more than 95% of patients.
  • C. Cholescintigraphy is used when ultrasound is equivocal for cholecystitis, so it is tempting as a confirming test, but it looks at the cystic duct and gallbladder and would delay drainage in a patient in septic shock.
  • D. Early cholecystectomy is the definitive treatment for acute cholecystitis, which makes it tempting with gallstones on ultrasound, but the infection here is in an obstructed common bile duct, and removing the gallbladder does not clear the stone from the duct.

Abdominal pain, jaundice and fever with chills form the Charcot triad, which is about 90% specific for acute cholangitis; adding confusion and hypotension makes the Reynolds pentad. A common bile duct wider than 6 mm with the gallbladder in place is dilated, and the high bilirubin and alkaline phosphatase point to extrahepatic obstruction, usually a duct stone. Merck calls acute cholangitis an emergency that needs aggressive supportive care, antibiotics such as piperacillin-tazobactam and urgent stone removal and drainage. Antibiotics and fluids buy time, but the infected bile stays under pressure until the duct is drained, which is why drainage, not a gallbladder procedure, treats the cause.

Sources: Merck Manual, Professional Version; Merck Manual Professional: Acute Cholecystitis (2025)
