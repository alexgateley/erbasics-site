Question 1

A 23-year-old who moved from Minnesota to Houston last week is treated in the ED for heat exhaustion after his second day on a roofing crew. He recovered with rest, cooling and IV 0.9% saline and is being discharged. He says he plans to return to work tomorrow. Which instruction about returning to work is MOST important?

  1. Work your full shift tomorrow, since today's symptoms have resolved
  2. Do the hardest work in the hottest hours so that your body adapts faster
  3. Build up time and effort in the heat gradually over 1 to 2 weeks
  4. Drink at least 4 L of water each shift to keep the heat from affecting you

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Resolution is reassuring, but full exposure the next day repeats the conditions that caused the episode in a worker who has not yet adapted to the heat.
  • B. Heat exposure is what drives acclimatization, which makes this tempting, but heavy work at peak heat before adapting is how heat illness happens; exposure is increased step by step.
  • C. Correct. Lack of heat acclimatization is a major risk factor, and acclimatization comes from gradually increasing the intensity and duration of work in the heat over about 7 to 14 days.
  • D. Regular drinking matters on a hot roof, which makes this tempting, but no volume of water replaces acclimatization, which comes only from gradually increasing time and effort in the heat; plain water without food or salt over long sweaty shifts can also lower the sodium.

The body adapts to heat through acclimatization: sweating starts earlier and becomes more efficient, plasma volume expands, and heart rate and core temperature rise less for the same work. The NATA position statement recommends acclimatizing gradually over 7 to 14 days by progressively increasing the intensity and duration of activity, and it notes that the first 2 to 3 weeks carry the highest risk and that the benefit decays within about 3 weeks without heat exposure. WMS 2024 adds that about 80% of acclimatization is gained in 7 days of 1 to 2 hours of moderate exertion in heat. Because he has had only 2 days in the heat and a recent episode of heat illness raises the risk of another, a full shift tomorrow repeats the exposure that caused this one.

Sources: Merck Manual Professional: Heat Exhaustion (2025); Merck Manual, Professional Version

Question 2

A house-fire victim remains hypotensive with lactate 12 mmol/L, SvO₂ 88%, PaO₂ 250 on high-flow O₂, and COHb 8%. Which treatment is MOST appropriate?

  1. Sodium bicarbonate
  2. Hydroxocobalamin
  3. Hyperbaric oxygen alone
  4. Methylene blue

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Bicarbonate does not address the cause, which is blocked mitochondrial oxygen use.
  • B. Correct. A very high lactate and high SvO₂ after smoke inhalation suggest cyanide toxicity. Hydroxocobalamin binds cyanide to form cyanocobalamin.
  • C. The COHb of 8% does not explain this degree of lactic acidosis, and oxygen alone will not reverse cyanide toxicity.
  • D. Methylene blue treats methemoglobinemia. It is not the antidote for cyanide.

Cyanide blocks cytochrome c oxidase, so cells cannot use oxygen (cytopathic hypoxia). Tissues extract little oxygen, producing an abnormally high SvO₂ and a severe lactic acidosis despite adequate delivery. In a smoke inhalation victim, hypotension or a lactate of 10 mmol/L or more strongly suggests cyanide. Hydroxocobalamin (5 g IV) is first-line. It turns urine and skin red and interferes with colorimetric labs and co-oximetry, so blood samples should be drawn first when possible.

Sources: AHA 2025 Part 10: Special Circumstances; Sheehy's Manual of Emergency Care, 8th ed.; Cyanokit (hydroxocobalamin) SmPC

Question 3

A 29-year-old stood close to an explosion that scattered powder from an industrial cesium-137 source. He has no injuries. His clothing has been removed and bagged and he has showered, and a whole-body survey with a thin-window Geiger-Muller probe now reads near background over his skin. He says he coughed in the dust cloud for several minutes and swallowed some of it. Which action should the nurse take NEXT?

  1. Wipe his nares and mouth with moistened swabs and save his urine and stool for counting
  2. Draw one absolute lymphocyte count now and plan care from that value
  3. Repeat the whole-body skin survey after a second shower
  4. Give Prussian blue once the dose estimate from his time to vomiting is known

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. A skin survey finds only external contamination, so swabs of the nares, ears, mouth and any wounds are counted, and urine, feces and emesis are tested, when inhaled or swallowed material is suspected.
  • B. The count is drawn, but a single early value means little; the differential is repeated at 24, 48 and 72 hours, and it estimates the dose from penetrating exposure rather than internal contamination.
  • C. A second survey confirms the skin result, which is already near background, and no amount of showering reaches material that is inside the body.
  • D. Prussian blue is the decorporation agent for cesium-137, which makes this tempting, but time to vomiting estimates the dose from whole-body irradiation, is imprecise with many false positives, and says nothing about how much cesium he took in; treating internal contamination starts with confirming it.

External contamination is radioactive material on the body, which a survey meter can find and a shower can remove; internal contamination is material taken in by inhalation, ingestion or a break in the skin, where it keeps emitting radiation until it is excreted or decays. Because a probe passed over the skin cannot detect it, the nares, ears, mouth and wounds are wiped with moistened swabs that are counted, and urine, feces and vomitus are tested. Decorporation then matches the radionuclide: Prussian blue exchanges ions in the gut for cesium-137, rubidium-82 and thallium-201, calcium or zinc DTPA chelates americium and plutonium, and potassium iodide saturates the thyroid for iodine isotopes. Serial complete blood counts with differential remain the dose and prognosis tool for whole-body irradiation, with the lowest lymphocyte count at 48 hours mapped to an estimated dose.

Sources: Merck Manual Professional: Radiation Exposure and Contamination (2025); HHS Radiation Emergency Medical Management (REMM)

Question 4

A 27-year-old hiked 3 miles out of a canyon in boots that had frozen solid and arrives at the ED with both feet white, hard and numb to the ankles. His core temperature is 36.4 °C (97.5 °F), he is not shivering, and IV opioids are ready. A colleague suggests leaving the feet frozen until the burn center accepts him in the morning. Which action is BEST?

  1. Rewarm both feet now in circulating water at 37 to 39 °C
  2. Keep the feet frozen in dry dressings until the burn center accepts him
  3. Hold chemical heat packs against each sole until the skin softens
  4. Massage the feet with warm hands before putting them in water

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. The longer tissue stays frozen the greater the damage, and he is now somewhere the thawed feet cannot refreeze or be walked on, so rapid rewarming in water tolerably warm to the touch starts at once.
  • B. Delaying thawing applies to a patient who must still walk to reach care, because thawed tissue tolerates walking poorly and refreezing causes worse injury; neither applies to a patient already in the department.
  • C. A heat pack seems a practical way to thaw tissue, but uncontrolled dry heat burns skin that cannot feel temperature, and the depth of injury grows with each burn.
  • D. Rubbing feels like it restores circulation, yet friction on frozen tissue full of ice crystals tears cells and adds mechanical injury to the freezing injury.

Ice crystals form in and between cells during freezing, and nearby unfrozen tissue is also at risk because vasoconstriction and thrombosis cause ischemic injury. Depth of loss tracks how long and how deeply the tissue stayed frozen, so thawing is not postponed once the patient is in a warm place: affected areas are immersed in water tolerably warm to the touch, 37 to 39 °C, usually for 15 to 30 minutes, with all jewelry removed first and parenteral opioids ready because rewarming is very painful. Delay is reserved for a patient who must walk any distance to care, and the frozen part is then cleansed, air dried and protected in dry bulky dressings. Dry heat sources and rubbing are both avoided. Ibuprofen is given for prostaglandin inhibition, the part is elevated and left open to warm air, and hemorrhagic blisters are left intact while large clear blisters are left alone or aspirated sterilely.

Sources: Merck Manual Professional: Frostbite (2026)

Question 5

A 19-year-old competitive swimmer was found at the bottom of a pool after practicing underwater laps. A teammate says he took many fast, deep breaths before each lap to stay under longer. He was resuscitated at the scene and is now awake on 4 L/min oxygen by nasal cannula with SpO₂ 96%. He says he never felt the need to come up. Which explanation should the nurse include in his teaching?

  1. Fast breathing made his blood acidic, which caused a seizure underwater
  2. Fast breathing lowers carbon dioxide, so oxygen can fall enough to cause blackout before the urge to breathe
  3. Breath-holding after fast breathing is safe if he surfaces at the first urge to breathe
  4. Fast breathing stores extra oxygen, so his blackout most likely came from a cramp

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Hyperventilation causes respiratory alkalosis, not acidosis, and the blackout is from hypoxia rather than a seizure.
  • B. Correct. The urge to breathe is driven mainly by rising carbon dioxide. Blowing it off beforehand delays that signal, so oxygen falls to the point of losing consciousness underwater without warning.
  • C. Stopping at the urge sounds safe, but hyperventilation delays the urge until after oxygen is critically low, which is how he lost consciousness without feeling it.
  • D. Hemoglobin is already nearly saturated at rest, so overbreathing adds little oxygen; the belief that it does is what makes the practice dangerous.

Merck describes dangerous underwater breath-holding behaviors, often in fit young men who swim well: intentional hyperventilation blows off carbon dioxide before submerged swimming, so hypoxia occurs first, then loss of consciousness, and then drowning. Because carbon dioxide, not oxygen, is the main stimulus to breathe, the swimmer gets no warning before blacking out, often called shallow-water or hypoxic blackout. Teaching covers stopping pre-dive hyperventilation and competitive breath-holding games, never practicing breath-holding alone, and having a supervisor who knows the risk. He still needs observation for aspiration lung injury after his resuscitation.

Sources: Merck Manual Professional: Drowning (2026)

Question 6

A 52-year-old with a rattlesnake bite to the calf is receiving crotalid antivenom for spreading swelling and a low platelet count. Fifteen minutes into the infusion he develops generalized hives, wheezing and throat tightness. Data are: HR 118, BP 88/54 mm Hg, RR 26, SpO₂ 91%. Which action should the nurse take FIRST?

  1. Continue the infusion, because his envenomation outweighs the reaction
  2. Stop the antivenom and give IM epinephrine per the anaphylaxis order
  3. Slow the infusion to 25 mL/h and give the ordered IV diphenhydramine
  4. Stop the antivenom and give the ordered IV diphenhydramine and methylprednisolone

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. The envenomation still needs treatment, which makes continuing tempting, but uncontrolled anaphylaxis is the more immediate threat to life; resuming is weighed only after the reaction is treated.
  • B. Correct. Hives with wheezing, throat tightness and hypotension is anaphylaxis. The infusion is stopped and epinephrine given at once; antihistamines, fluids and a later decision about resuming antivenom follow.
  • C. Slowing the rate is how mild rate-related infusion reactions are managed, but a reaction with airway and blood-pressure involvement needs the infusion stopped and epinephrine.
  • D. Stopping is right, and antihistamines and steroids are adjuncts, but neither treats bronchospasm or shock quickly; leaving out epinephrine in anaphylaxis is the error.

The CroFab label says to discontinue the infusion and give emergency treatment for an acute hypersensitivity reaction, with epinephrine, IV antihistamines and albuterol kept ready. The unified algorithm says to halt the infusion and give antihistamines, corticosteroids and fluids as needed, with epinephrine for severe reactions; this patient's wheeze and hypotension make it severe. Because the envenomation still needs treatment, whether to resume antivenom is a risk-benefit decision the panel leaves to expert consultation, usually with a toxicologist or poison center, rather than an automatic restart. The label also starts each infusion slowly for the first 10 minutes so reactions can be caught early.

Sources: Lavonas et al., Unified Treatment Algorithm for Crotaline Snakebite (2011); CroFab (Crotalidae Polyvalent Immune Fab) Prescribing Information; Merck Manual Professional: Snakebites (2025)

Question 7

A cook splashed hot oil on the palm and fingers of one hand 5 minutes ago, causing blisters. He is wearing a wedding ring, and his vital signs are normal. What is the BEST initial wound care?

  1. Open the blisters to drain them, then apply a dry dressing and transport
  2. Soak it in iced water for 20 minutes, with the ring on until swelling settles
  3. Apply a cooling gel, then wrap the hand firmly to protect blisters
  4. Remove the ring, cool with running water, then cover with a dry dressing

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. Blisters are left intact, and early cooling is skipped.
  • B. Tempting for pain, but iced water can worsen the burn, and swelling makes the ring harder to remove.
  • C. Tempting because gels are sold for burns, but gels and ointments are not applied, and firm wraps constrict.
  • D. Correct. Jewelry comes off early, cooling is still useful, and a dry cover limits contamination.

The guideline's first steps are to stop the burning, remove jewelry, leave blisters intact, cover with a dry dressing or clean sheet, and avoid gels and ointments. A BMJ review adds that running tepid water for up to 20 minutes is effective when started soon after injury, and warns against iced water. Cooling is kept to the burn so the patient does not get cold. Pain management is critical, and hand involvement makes this a candidate for burn center transport, even at this small size. MedlinePlus classes second-degree hand burns as major.

Sources: NASEMSO Model EMS Guidelines v3.0 (2022); MedlinePlus Medical Encyclopedia: Burns (2026); Hudspith 2004: First Aid and Treatment of Minor Burns

Question 8

A patient with delirium has received scheduled haloperidol for 5 days. The patient now has T 40.2°C, generalized “lead-pipe” rigidity, diaphoresis, labile BP, mutism, and CK of 18,000 units/L. Reflexes are normal, without clonus. Which condition is MOST likely?

  1. Anticholinergic toxicity
  2. Malignant hyperthermia
  3. Neuroleptic malignant syndrome (NMS)
  4. Serotonin syndrome (serotonin toxicity)

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Anticholinergic toxicity causes dry, flushed skin, mydriasis, and urinary retention, not diaphoresis and rigidity.
  • B. Malignant hyperthermia is triggered by volatile anesthetics or succinylcholine during anesthesia.
  • C. Correct. Hyperthermia, lead-pipe rigidity, autonomic instability, altered mental status, and elevated CK after a dopamine antagonist define NMS.
  • D. Serotonin syndrome develops within hours and features clonus, hyperreflexia, tremor, and GI symptoms rather than lead-pipe rigidity.

Neuroleptic malignant syndrome is caused by dopamine D2 blockade (antipsychotics or antiemetics such as metoclopramide) or by abrupt withdrawal of dopaminergic drugs. It develops over days, with hyperthermia, lead-pipe rigidity, autonomic instability, altered mental status, and rhabdomyolysis. Treatment is to stop the offending agent, provide aggressive supportive care (cooling, fluids, preventing AKI), give benzodiazepines, and consider dantrolene and bromocriptine for severe cases.

Sources: AACN Core Curriculum, 8th ed.; Ferri's Clinical Advisor 2025

Question 9

A 34-year-old man had 3 days of fever to 40 °C (104 °F), severe headache, back pain and prostration before a rash appeared 2 days ago. The lesions are deep, firm, round pustules, all at the same stage, densest on his face and forearms and present on his palms and soles, with fewer on his trunk. Data are: T 38.9 °C (102 °F), HR 116, BP 108/68 mm Hg. Which action should the nurse take FIRST?

  1. Isolate him with airborne and contact precautions, and notify public health now
  2. Isolate him with airborne and contact precautions, assigning a nurse who has had chickenpox
  3. Isolate him with airborne and contact precautions, and report him if the varicella PCR is negative
  4. Isolate him with droplet and contact precautions, and send vesicle fluid for viral culture

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. A febrile prodrome followed by deep, firm lesions at the same stage in a centrifugal pattern meets high-risk smallpox criteria. He goes to an airborne infection isolation room, and infection control and the health department are notified immediately.
  • B. Matching staff immunity to the exposure is a sound habit, but immunity to varicella does not protect against smallpox, and assigning by chickenpox history skips the immediate notification a possible smallpox case needs.
  • C. The isolation is right, but this pattern does not fit chickenpox, and waiting for a varicella result delays the public health response that a possible smallpox case needs at once.
  • D. Viral testing will be needed, but smallpox spreads by the airborne route as well as by contact, so droplet precautions leave staff unprotected, and testing does not replace immediate notification.

CDC's major criteria for smallpox are a febrile prodrome 1 to 4 days before the rash, classic deep-seated, firm, round vesicles or pustules, and lesions at the same stage of development on any one part of the body; minor criteria include a centrifugal distribution densest on the face and distal limbs and lesions on the palms and soles. CDC's list of smallpox look-alikes notes that chickenpox is most common in children under 10 and that children usually have no prodrome, so an adult with a toxic prodrome and same-stage centrifugal lesions is treated as possible smallpox. For a suspected case, CDC directs airborne infection isolation with N95 respirators, gowns and gloves, keeping the patient out of waiting areas, and immediate notification of infection control and the health department.

Sources: CDC Isolation Precautions Guideline (2007); CDC: Smallpox Diagnosis and Evaluation (2024); CDC: Clinical Signs and Symptoms of Smallpox (2024)

Question 10

A 2-year-old in Houston stepped on a mound in the yard 18 hours ago and has about 30 stings across both feet and ankles. Each lesion is now a small pustule on a red base, and she keeps scratching them. She is afebrile, feeding normally, and has no hives away from the stings, no wheeze and no swelling of the lips or tongue. Her mother asks whether the pus means infection. Which plan is MOST appropriate?

  1. Oral cephalexin for 7 days because of the number of lesions
  2. Unroofing and draining each pustule, then antibiotic ointment
  3. Cool compresses, an oral antihistamine, and the pustules left closed
  4. An epinephrine autoinjector and referral for venom immunotherapy

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Many lesions suggest many portals for infection, yet these are a venom reaction rather than cellulitis, and she has no fever or spreading redness to treat.
  • B. Pus invites drainage, but these pustules are sterile, and opening 30 of them creates the breaks in the skin through which infection and even sepsis can follow.
  • C. Correct. Fire ant stings predictably form sterile pustules that break down over the next 1 to 3 days, so the lesions are kept intact and clean while itching is treated and the parents watch for infection.
  • D. Immunotherapy is highly effective after fire ant anaphylaxis, which makes the referral sound prudent, but she has had no systemic allergic signs for it to prevent.

Fire ants of the genus Solenopsis are established across the southern United States and are especially dense in the Gulf region, where in urban areas they may sting as much as 40% of the population. The ant bites to anchor itself and then stings repeatedly as it pivots, leaving a central bite partly encircled by a reddened sting line. Immediate pain is followed by a wheal and flare that often fades within 45 minutes and gives rise to a sterile pustule, which breaks down over about 30 to 70 hours; some children instead develop an edematous, erythematous, itchy lesion. Because the pustule is sterile, care is symptomatic, and the risk comes from scratching or unroofing lesions, which can lead to secondary infection and occasionally sepsis. Anaphylaxis from fire ant stings is probably rare, although mononeuritis and seizures have been reported, and venom immunotherapy is reserved for children who have had a systemic reaction.

Sources: Merck Manual, Professional Version
