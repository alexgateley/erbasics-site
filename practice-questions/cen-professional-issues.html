Question 1

Parents bring their 19-day-old daughter, born at term, because she felt warm. She is feeding well, has a strong cry and pink skin. Data are: rectal T 38.3 °C (100.9 °F), HR 168, RR 48, SpO2 99%. Which ESI level should the triage nurse assign?

  1. ESI level 2
  2. ESI level 4, as she looks well and her vital signs are normal for age
  3. ESI level 1, as a febrile neonate needs antibiotics within minutes
  4. ESI level 3, as she will need labs and IV antibiotics

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. ESI v5 assigns at least level 2 to an infant 28 days old or younger with a temperature over 38 °C (100.4 °F), however well the infant looks.
  • B. Her heart and respiratory rates are within the v5 limits for under 1 month, but a well appearance does not rule out serious bacterial infection in a neonate.
  • C. Antibiotics are urgent but are not on the ESI list of lifesaving interventions, and she has no airway, breathing or perfusion failure.
  • D. She will use two or more resources, but her age and fever already meet a point B high-risk rule, which comes before resource counting.

ESI v5 treats fever in the youngest infants as high risk regardless of appearance: from birth to 28 days, a temperature over 38 °C (100.4 °F) means at least level 2; from 1 to 3 months the nurse considers level 2. Its pediatric chapter also calls a temperature over 38 °C under 90 days, or under 36 °C at any age, a red flag. The AAP 2021 guideline on well-appearing febrile infants 8 to 60 days old, defined by a temperature of 38.0 °C or more, splits them into three age groups (8 to 21, 22 to 28 and 29 to 60 days), each with its own algorithm. Her heart rate is under the v5 limit of 190 for her age.

Sources: ENA ESI Handbook, 5th ed.; AAP Febrile Infant Guideline (2021)

Question 2

A 29-year-old dies in the ED after a motorcycle crash. The organ procurement organization (OPO) finds him medically suitable for tissue and eye donation. Its coordinator is 3 hours away, so it asks the hospital to have a designated requester speak with his parents, who are in the family room. Which member of the ED team may make the request?

  1. The nurse who completed the OPO-approved requester course
  2. The chaplain who has stayed with the parents
  3. The attending physician who led the resuscitation and told the parents of his death
  4. The charge nurse, who has helped many grieving families

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. A designated requester must have completed a course offered or approved by the OPO on how to approach families and request donation.
  • B. The chaplain's rapport is valuable for support, but without the OPO-approved course the chaplain cannot be the person who requests donation.
  • C. The physician knows the case best, but rank and involvement do not make someone a designated requester; the training does.
  • D. Experience with grieving families helps, but federal rules tie the role to completing the OPO course, not to years of practice.

Under 42 CFR 482.45, the hospital must work with the OPO so that every potential donor's family learns its options to donate organs, tissues or eyes, or to decline, and the person who makes the request must be an organ procurement representative or a designated requester. A designated requester has completed a course offered or approved by the OPO and designed with the tissue and eye bank community. The rule also asks for discretion and sensitivity to the family's circumstances, views and beliefs, and requires agreements with at least one tissue bank and one eye bank. Wojda and colleagues found authorization more likely when a trained requester approaches the family with a member of the care team.

Sources: 42 CFR 482.45: Organ, Tissue and Eye Procurement; Wojda et al.: Keys to Successful Organ Procurement (2017)

Question 3

A 27-year-old, gravida 2 para 1 at 39 weeks, walks into an ED whose hospital has no obstetric unit. She has contractions every 4 minutes, and the cervix is 5 cm dilated. Data are: BP 118/74 mm Hg, HR 92, fetal heart rate 140 with accelerations. She asks to drive herself to the hospital where she is booked, 30 minutes away. Which statement about her care is MOST accurate?

  1. Her request turns the departure into a discharge, which falls outside EMTALA
  2. She may leave by car once a nurse confirms false labor with one cervical exam
  3. She is stable for transfer, since her vital signs and the fetal heart rate are normal
  4. She is unstable while in labor, so a transfer must meet the EMTALA transfer rules

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. A written request is one way to authorize a transfer, but the move must still be an appropriate transfer, and she must be told the risks and benefits first.
  • B. False labor can be certified by a physician, certified nurse-midwife or other qualified medical person, but only after a reasonable time of observation; a single exam cannot show that, and regular contractions with the cervix at 5 cm point to true labor.
  • C. Normal maternal and fetal findings are reassuring clinically, but for a woman in labor EMTALA defines stabilized as having delivered the child and the placenta.
  • D. Correct. Labor continues through delivery of the placenta, so she has an unstabilized emergency condition, and a transfer needs a certification or written request plus an appropriate transfer.

EMTALA defines labor as the process of childbirth from the latent phase through delivery of the placenta, and a woman with contractions is presumed to be in true labor unless a physician, certified nurse-midwife or other qualified medical person certifies false labor after a reasonable time of observation. For a pregnant woman, stabilized means that the child and the placenta have been delivered, and contractions are an emergency condition when there is not enough time for a safe transfer before delivery or the transfer threatens the woman or the fetus. So her request does not end the hospital's duty: she is informed of the risks, and any transfer needs certification or her written request, an accepting hospital, records, and qualified personnel and transport equipment.

Sources: EMTALA, 42 CFR 489.24

Question 4

An ED is notified of a commuter train derailment, and about 40 casualties are expected within the hour. The hospital's command structure has not yet been set up, and within minutes the ED medical director, the house supervisor and two nurse managers are each giving the charge nurse different instructions about where to place arriving patients. Which action BEST resolves this problem?

  1. Activate hospital incident command, with one incident commander and assigned roles
  2. Let each leader run the area that matches his or her usual job until the surge ends
  3. Follow the ED medical director, as the most senior clinician present in the department
  4. Call a meeting of the four leaders to agree on a bed for each patient as patients arrive

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. Incident command gives one person overall authority and assigns every other leader a defined role, so each staff member takes direction from a single supervisor.
  • B. Dividing the department by habit keeps several independent chains of command and no one coordinating beds, staff and supplies across the whole response.
  • C. Seniority sounds like a tiebreaker, but it leaves the other three leaders still issuing orders; the problem is the missing command structure, not who outranks whom.
  • D. Consensus by committee is slow and cannot keep pace with 40 arrivals; the incident commander sets objectives and delegates instead of deciding each placement in a meeting.

The incident command system is the national standard for organizing a disaster response, and hospitals use their own version, the Hospital Incident Command System. Its core principles fix exactly this problem. Unity of command means each participant reports to only one supervisor, which increases accountability and prevents conflicting instructions; information can flow in any direction, but instructions flow from the top down. Span of control keeps each supervisor responsible for about 3 to 7 people, and the structure expands by adding sections (operations, planning, logistics, finance and administration), branches and units rather than adding independent bosses. Here, four leaders acting at once is a command failure, so the department needs one incident commander and assigned roles.

Sources: StatPearls (NCBI Bookshelf); StatPearls: EMS Incident Command System (2024)

Question 5

A 50-year-old triaged as ESI 3 for nausea and upper abdominal pain has been in the waiting room for 40 minutes. She comes back to the triage desk saying she now has chest pressure and is sweating. Which action is BEST?

  1. Repeat her vital signs and raise her level if they cross the high-risk limits
  2. Obtain an ECG at triage and raise her level if the provider reads it as abnormal
  3. Raise her to ESI 2, arrange placement for an ECG now and document the change in condition
  4. Keep her at ESI 3, since a level stays as assigned, and send her for an ECG

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Vital signs are checked at point D for stable patients, but new chest pressure with diaphoresis is already a point B finding that does not depend on the numbers.
  • B. An abnormal ECG would confirm level 2, but the presentation itself is suspicious for acute coronary syndrome, so waiting for the reading delays placement.
  • C. Correct. A change in condition before placement allows an upward change in level, and chest pressure suspicious for acute coronary syndrome is a high-risk ESI 2 situation.
  • D. ESI v5 does say an assigned level is not changed, but it makes an exception when the patient's condition changes before placement from the waiting area.

ESI v5 allows the triage nurse to change a level when the patient's condition changes before placement from the waiting area, and the only change it permits is an increase, because the patient may now be the highest priority in the department. The record must show the change in condition, the reason for the new level and the nurse's resulting action. Active chest pain suspicious for acute coronary syndrome is one of v5's high-risk examples at point B, so the suspicion alone meets level 2, and v5 sets a goal of an ECG within 10 minutes for chest pain. v5 also notes that cardiac events may present with nausea, vomiting, fatigue or weakness rather than classic chest pain, and asks nurses to pay close attention to female patients.

Sources: ENA ESI Handbook, 5th ed.

Question 6

A 25-year-old was shot in the thigh, and the emergency physician removes a bullet lying just under the skin and hands it to the nurse. An officer is expected within the hour. Which action by the nurse is BEST?

  1. Put it in its own container, then seal it, label it and record each hand-off on a custody form
  2. Place it in the paper bag with his clothing, so his evidence stays together in one place
  3. Leave it in a specimen cup on the bedside tray until the officer comes to collect it
  4. Hand it to the officer on arrival without paperwork, since police then take custody

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. A separate sealed container labeled with the patient, collector, date and time, plus a custody form signed at every transfer, keeps the bullet usable as evidence.
  • B. One bag sounds organized, but each projectile goes in its own container; mixing it with the clothing invites transfer between items and loss.
  • C. The cup keeps it visible, but an unsealed item left unattended in a busy room cannot be accounted for, which breaks the chain of custody.
  • D. Police do take custody, but the transfer itself must be recorded with a signature; an undocumented hand-off leaves a gap in the chain.

Projectiles removed from a patient are evidence and are never discarded. Nursing forensic guidance recommends handling them carefully, preferably with tweezers, recording where each was recovered, and storing each projectile and each weapon in its own container cushioned with gauze, labeled with the patient's information. Containers are sealed with tape and labeled with the collector's name and the date and time of collection, and a chain of custody form records every transfer by signature. Merck describes the same principle for forensic specimens: individually packaged, labeled, dated and sealed, with custody documented, because a gap in who held the item can make it unusable in court.

Sources: Silva et al., Preservation of Forensic Traces (2022); Merck Manual Professional: Medical Examination of the Sexual Assault Victim (2026)

Question 7

A 28-year-old triaged as ESI 4 for a wrist injury has been seen by a nurse practitioner in the fast-track area, who ordered a radiograph, labs and IV analgesia. The fast-track nurse asks whether the triage level should be changed to reflect the added care. What is the BEST response?

  1. Update it at discharge, when the total resources used are known
  2. Ask the nurse practitioner to assign a second ESI level that reflects current acuity
  3. Leave the level as assigned, since ESI is not re-scored after provider evaluation
  4. Change it to ESI 3, since the care now includes more than one resource type

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Matching the level to resources used sounds accurate, but ESI is a judgment made at presentation and is not used for billing or as a running acuity record.
  • B. Trained providers may triage at the first encounter, but ESI v5 says a provider should not assign a second level after the nurse has triaged the patient.
  • C. Correct. ESI v5 states that it is not appropriate to change the ESI level once a physician or advanced practice provider has seen the patient.
  • D. Resource counts are predictions made at the first encounter; care that turns out larger than predicted does not reopen the triage decision.

ESI is a summative judgment made at the initial encounter to predict the risk of decompensation and the resources a patient will need. ESI v5 says it is not an ongoing measure of acuity, so the level is not changed once a physician or advanced practice provider has seen the patient, and a provider should not assign a second level after nurse triage. Before placement, a change in condition can raise the level with documentation. After provider evaluation, a deteriorating patient is escalated through the usual care team rather than re-triaged, and ESI levels are not used for billing.

Sources: ENA ESI Handbook, 5th ed.

Question 8

During a respiratory pandemic, the state has declared crisis standards of care and the hospital has activated its published ventilator allocation protocol. Every ventilator is in use. In the ED, a 71-year-old who arrived 40 minutes ago and a 34-year-old who just arrived both need intubation. The ED physician asks the nurse to help decide which patient gets the next ventilator. Which response by the nurse is BEST?

  1. Give it to whichever patient the ED physician judges sicker at the bedside
  2. Give it to the 34-year-old, since the protocol favors the younger patient
  3. Refer both patients to the triage team that applies the protocol's criteria
  4. Give it to the 71-year-old, since he arrived first and arrival order is the fairest rule

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Clinical judgment matters, but under crisis standards an individual clinician's choice in the moment is exactly what a published protocol and triage team replace.
  • B. Protocols weigh likelihood of benefit, usually from prognosis scores, rather than age alone; and the bedside team should not be applying the protocol itself.
  • C. Correct. Allocation protocols are applied by triage officers or committees outside direct patient care, which keeps decisions consistent and spares the bedside team an impossible choice.
  • D. First-come, first-served seems neutral, but in an urgent shortage it rewards proximity and luck; pandemic frameworks reject it in favor of maximizing benefits.

Once crisis standards of care are declared, scarce ventilators are allocated by the hospital's published protocol rather than by the usual one-patient-at-a-time decisions. Pandemic allocation frameworks give the most weight to maximizing benefits (saving the most lives, or the most life-years), advise against first-come, first-served allocation and use random selection only between patients with similar prognoses. Leaving these decisions to individual front-line clinicians places an acute and lasting emotional toll on them, so institutions use triage officers, physicians outside direct patient care, or committees of experienced physicians and ethicists to apply the guidelines. The nurse's role is to escalate to that team, give both patients full supportive care while the decision is made, and document.

Sources: Johnstone, Bioethics, 7th ed.; Emanuel et al.: Fair Allocation of Scarce Medical Resources in the Time of Covid-19 (NEJM 2020)

Question 9

At 0300 a fire on the floor above the ED sends smoke into the east treatment hallway, where four patients are on stretchers. The west half of the ED, beyond the smoke compartment doors, is clear, and the fire department is on scene. No building evacuation has been ordered. Where should the charge nurse move the patients in the east hallway?

  1. Down the stairwell to the basement level, away from the fire above the ED
  2. By elevator to an unaffected inpatient floor in the hospital's other tower
  3. Through the smoke doors into the clear west half, closing the doors behind them
  4. Out through the ambulance bay to the parking lot, away from the building

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Vertical evacuation to another floor is the next step only when staying on the floor is unsafe; the west compartment is clear, so carrying stretcher patients down stairs adds risk without need.
  • B. Moving patients to another building or floor comes after horizontal movement, and elevators may be unsafe or out of service in a fire, so evacuation stairwells are used for vertical moves.
  • C. Correct. Horizontal evacuation to an adjacent smoke compartment on the same floor is the first move and protects patients in most fires; the closed doors hold the smoke back.
  • D. Leaving the building feels safest, but a building-wide evacuation is ordered only when it is unsafe to remain inside, and it exposes unstable patients to the cold and to delays in care.

Hospitals are built in smoke compartments separated by fire-rated doors so that patients can be defended in place. Hospital evacuation plans describe a hierarchy: horizontal evacuation from a threatened area to a non-threatened area on the same floor first, which assures life safety in most situations; horizontal movement into an adjacent building second; vertical evacuation to another floor when it is unsafe or undesirable to remain; and a building-wide evacuation to the exterior, ordered only when it is unsafe or undesirable to remain in the building, last. Vertical moves use evacuation stairwells because elevators may be unsafe or nonfunctional. Here the west compartment is clear, so the charge nurse moves the four patients through the smoke doors and closes them, then accounts for every patient.

Sources: Sheehy's Emergency Nursing, 7th ed.; UCLA Health Fire Safety: Evacuation Types

Question 10

A 66-year-old was triaged as ESI 3 for dizziness and vomiting 45 minutes ago. At a waiting-room recheck she veers to the left when she tries to walk to the bathroom and says she is seeing double. Her face is symmetric, her arm strength is equal and her speech is clear. Data are: BP 172/94 mm Hg, HR 88, RR 18, SpO2 97%, fingerstick glucose 128 mg/dL. Which action is BEST?

  1. Give the standing-order antiemetic and recheck her gait in 30 minutes
  2. Re-level her to ESI 1 so she can go straight to CT
  3. Up-triage her to ESI 2 and activate the stroke alert
  4. Keep her at ESI 3, as her face, arm and speech screen is normal

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Treating nausea fits a peripheral cause of vertigo, but new neurologic signs need a stroke evaluation now, and a recheck delays time-dependent treatment.
  • B. She needs a CT quickly, but a stroke CT is diagnostic rather than lifesaving, and she has no airway, breathing or perfusion failure.
  • C. Correct. New imbalance and double vision are the balance and eyes signs of BE-FAST, and stroke signs are a level 2 presentation; the level may be raised because her condition changed before placement.
  • D. A normal face, arm and speech check is reassuring for an anterior stroke, but it misses many posterior strokes, which show up as dizziness, vomiting, imbalance and visual change.

BE-FAST adds balance and eyes to the face, arm and speech checks of FAST. In one stroke center review, FAST missed 14.1% of ischemic strokes and BE-FAST 4.4%, and posterior strokes were missed at first more often than anterior ones (37% versus 16%), often because they present as dizziness or vomiting. ESI v5 places stroke signs at decision point B as level 2; a CT is a diagnostic study, so the stroke alert does not need level 1. A triage level can be changed only upward and only before placement, and the nurse documents the change in condition, the reason for the new level and the action taken. Her normal glucose rules out hypoglycemia as a mimic.

Sources: ENA ESI Handbook, 5th ed.; ASA: Stroke Symptoms (BE FAST)
