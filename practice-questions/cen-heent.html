Question 1

A 17-year-old had a sore throat 8 days ago that was getting better. For 2 days he has had high fever with rigors, pain and swelling along the left side of the neck below the jaw, and sharp chest pain on breathing in. He does not use drugs. Data are: T 39.8 °C (103.6 °F), HR 124/min, BP 96/58 mm Hg, RR 26/min, SpO2 92% on room air; a chest radiograph shows several small nodules in both lungs. Which plan should the nurse anticipate?

  1. A CT pulmonary angiogram and a heparin infusion for venous thromboembolism
  2. An echocardiogram for right-sided endocarditis as the source of the emboli
  3. Oral azithromycin and a monospot test for atypical pneumonia after mononucleosis
  4. A contrast CT of the neck and IV antibiotics that cover anaerobes

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. Pleuritic pain, tachycardia and hypoxemia suggest pulmonary embolism, but fever with rigors and multiple nodules are septic emboli, and anticoagulation is not the main treatment.
  • B. Septic lung emboli suggest tricuspid endocarditis, especially in people who inject drugs, but he does not, and the neck findings after pharyngitis point to the jugular vein.
  • C. Mononucleosis is common at his age and can precede Lemierre, but he is septic, and the usual anaerobe is often not susceptible to erythromycin, so an oral macrolide is a poor choice.
  • D. Correct. Pharyngitis followed days later by sepsis, lateral neck pain and lung nodules suggests Lemierre syndrome, septic thrombophlebitis of the internal jugular vein; contrast CT confirms it.

Lemierre syndrome typically affects healthy young people (median age about 21 in one systematic review): an oropharyngeal infection, often already resolving, is followed several days later by septic thrombophlebitis of the internal jugular vein, most often from the anaerobe Fusobacterium necrophorum. Tiwari's review describes pain and swelling along the sternocleidomastoid and septic pulmonary emboli with pleuritic pain, and names contrast CT as the diagnostic standard. Treatment is a beta-lactam or carbapenem, usually with metronidazole, for 3 to 5 weeks; Fusobacterium is less likely to be sensitive to erythromycin, and anticoagulation remains controversial, so the plan the nurse anticipates does not hinge on it. The nurse draws blood cultures before antibiotics, starts the sepsis bundle and reports the neck findings.

Sources: Tiwari, Lemierre's Syndrome in the 21st Century (2023); Merck Manual Professional: Parapharyngeal Abscess (2026)

Question 2

A 22-year-old struck his mouth on the edge of a swimming pool. An upper central incisor is broken across the crown with a small red spot that bleeds in the center of the fracture, and he winces when air touches it. The inside of the lower lip is swollen with a 1 cm laceration, and the missing piece of tooth was not found. Before the lip is repaired, which study should the nurse anticipate?

  1. A soft-tissue radiograph of the lower lip for the fragment
  2. No imaging; repair the lip now and arrange routine dental follow-up in 1 to 2 weeks
  3. A CT of the facial bones with 3-D reconstruction
  4. A panoramic radiograph to look for a fracture of the mandible

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. When a fragment is missing and there is a lip or cheek wound, radiographs of the soft tissue look for tooth fragments and debris before the wound is closed.
  • B. Closing the wound without looking risks sealing a fragment inside the lip, and an exposed pulp needs prompt dental treatment to preserve it rather than routine follow-up.
  • C. CT is used for suspected facial fractures, but nothing here suggests a facial fracture; the guideline images this injury with dental radiographs plus a soft-tissue film of the lip.
  • D. A facial blow makes jaw imaging tempting, but nothing here suggests a mandible fracture, such as malocclusion or a step-off; the missing fragment is the specific concern.

A crown fracture that exposes the pulp, seen as a red or bleeding point on the fracture surface that is sensitive to air, cold and sweets, is a complicated crown fracture (Ellis class III). When a fragment is missing and there is a soft-tissue wound, the IADT recommends radiographs of the lip or cheek to search for tooth fragments and foreign material, so that nothing is sutured inside the lip. The dentist then preserves the pulp with pulp capping or a partial pulpotomy, which matters most in immature teeth, and a recovered fragment can be rehydrated in water or saline for 20 minutes and bonded back.

Sources: IADT Guidelines: Fractures and Luxations (2020); Sheehy's Manual of Emergency Care, 8th ed.; ENA Core Curriculum, 8th ed.

Question 3

A 24-year-old cyclist is brought in after a crash with a GCS of 13 and facial abrasions. A bystander found one of his permanent upper incisors in the grass about 15 minutes ago, and the team cannot safely replant it while his level of consciousness is being assessed. How should the nurse store the tooth until the dentist arrives?

  1. Soaked in chlorhexidine mouth rinse to disinfect the root
  2. Wrapped in dry sterile gauze inside a labeled specimen cup
  3. Submerged in a cup of milk
  4. Placed in a cup of tap water

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Chlorhexidine is used to clean the injured mouth and as a home rinse after replantation; it is not a storage medium, and the root should be handled as little as possible.
  • B. Keeping the tooth clean seems sensible, but the root surface starts to dehydrate within minutes, and after about 30 minutes dry most ligament cells are no longer viable.
  • C. Correct. Milk heads the IADT list of storage media (milk, Hanks' balanced salt solution, saliva, saline) because it keeps the root's ligament cells from drying out.
  • D. Water is better than leaving the tooth dry, which makes this tempting, but the guideline calls it a poor medium; milk, Hanks' solution, saliva or saline are preferred when available.

An avulsed permanent tooth depends on the periodontal ligament cells covering its root, and those cells die as the root dries: after about 30 minutes of extra-oral dry time most are non-viable, and after 60 minutes the outlook is poor. Immediate replantation is best; when it cannot be done, as in a patient whose level of consciousness is in question, the tooth goes into a storage medium right away, in descending order of preference milk, Hanks' balanced salt solution, saliva or saline. Handle the tooth by the crown, not the root, and record how long it was out of the mouth and how it was kept, because the dentist uses both to plan treatment.

Sources: IADT Guidelines: Avulsion (2020); Sheehy's Manual of Emergency Care, 8th ed.; ENA Core Curriculum, 8th ed.

Question 4

A 62-year-old needs closed reduction of a displaced wrist fracture. He had surgery for a retinal detachment 10 days ago and wears a wristband stating that his left eye contains a gas bubble. Which planned medication should the nurse question?

  1. Fentanyl IV for procedural analgesia
  2. Lidocaine 1% hematoma block
  3. Nitrous oxide 50% by demand mask
  4. Ondansetron IV before the procedure

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Opioids can cause nausea, which makes them tempting to question after eye surgery, but nausea can be treated and fentanyl does not affect the gas bubble.
  • B. A hematoma block is a common way to numb a distal radius fracture and has no effect on the eye or the gas bubble.
  • C. Correct. Correct to question. Nitrous oxide diffuses rapidly into the intraocular gas and expands the bubble; the eye pressure can climb high enough to cause permanent vision loss.
  • D. Adding a drug may seem unnecessary, but preventing vomiting protects a recently operated eye, because retching raises the pressure inside it.

After pneumatic retinopexy or vitrectomy for retinal detachment, the eye may hold an expansile gas such as SF6 or C3F8, which can last up to about 2 months. Nitrous oxide diffuses quickly into the bubble and enlarges it in proportion to the concentration used, which can raise the pressure in the eye high enough to cause permanent vision loss, including reported retinal artery occlusions; anesthesia with nitrous oxide is contraindicated while any gas remains. Patients may wear an alert bracelet, but it is not always present, so ask about recent eye surgery. Severe eye pain after surgery can also trigger the oculocardiac reflex, with bradycardia and hypotension.

Sources: Elisha et al., Nurse Anesthesia, 7th ed.; Sheehy's Manual of Emergency Care, 8th ed.

Question 5

Four adults arrive at triage with nosebleeds. Which patient should be seen FIRST?

  1. A 62-year-old with blood pressure 178/96 mm Hg holding pressure on a steady drip
  2. A 30-year-old with dry nasal mucosa and a slow ooze from one nostril
  3. A 45-year-old on aspirin whose bleeding stopped with pressure an hour ago
  4. A 70-year-old spitting blood, pale, with a pulse of 122/min

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. Hypertension is a comorbidity that raises concern and draws attention, but this patient is controlling an anterior drip and shows no airway or perfusion threat.
  • B. A unilateral anterior ooze from dry mucosa is the typical low-acuity nosebleed that responds to compression.
  • C. Antiplatelet use raises the risk of rebleeding, but the bleeding has stopped and nothing suggests instability.
  • D. Correct. Blood from the mouth suggests a posterior or large-volume bleed threatening the airway, and pallor with tachycardia signals hypovolemia.

The AAO-HNS guideline asks the clinician first to separate nosebleed patients who need prompt management from those who do not. Bleeding from both sides of the nose or from the mouth, suspected large-volume or prolonged bleeding, and signs of hypovolemia such as tachycardia, syncope or orthostatic hypotension call for immediate care, because the threats are airway compromise from blood in the oropharynx and hemodynamic instability. Blood in the mouth also suggests a posterior source, which is more common in older adults and harder to control. Hypertension, antithrombotic drugs and comorbid disease raise risk but rank below active instability.

Sources: AAO-HNS Guideline: Nosebleed (2020); ENA ESI Handbook, 5th ed.; ENA Core Curriculum, 8th ed.

Question 6

A 3-year-old who had a cold last week now has fever, drooling and a muffled voice and refuses to extend the neck. A lateral neck radiograph shows widened prevertebral soft tissue, and a contrast CT of the neck is ordered. Which nursing action is MOST appropriate?

  1. Offer cold juice and oral ibuprofen to ease swallowing before the scan
  2. Lay the child flat and restrain both arms to draw blood cultures
  3. Inspect the back of the throat with a tongue blade to look for a bulge
  4. Have a clinician skilled in pediatric airway management go with the child to CT

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. Fluids and analgesia seem kind, but a drooling child cannot manage secretions and may need drainage under anesthesia, so nothing is given by mouth.
  • B. Blood cultures are part of the workup, but agitation can worsen early airway obstruction; blood draws are postponed if they distress the child.
  • C. Seeing a posterior pharyngeal bulge would support the diagnosis, but probing can rupture the abscess and cause aspiration; any examination is done cautiously by an airway-skilled clinician with suction ready.
  • D. Correct. A retropharyngeal abscess can obstruct the airway, and lying supine in the scanner may tip a child into distress, so someone able to manage the airway goes along.

Retropharyngeal abscess mostly affects children under 5, whose retropharyngeal lymph nodes suppurate after an upper respiratory infection; the nodes regress by about age 5. Fever, drooling, muffled voice, torticollis and pain on neck extension are typical, unlike epiglottitis, where the child extends the neck. A lateral neck film shows widened prevertebral tissue (over 7 mm at C2), and contrast CT confirms it. Care is IV antibiotics covering streptococci, Staphylococcus aureus and anaerobes, otolaryngology consultation, and drainage when needed, with the airway watched throughout.

Sources: StatPearls (NCBI Bookshelf); ENA ENPC Provider Manual, 6th ed.; Wong's Care of Infants & Children, 12th ed.

Question 7

A 62-year-old with acute angle closure of the left eye received timolol and brimonidine drops and acetazolamide 500 mg IV 1 hour ago, then two doses of pilocarpine 15 minutes apart. She is still vomiting, and the eye pressure is 50 mm Hg. Data are: BP 148/86 mm Hg, HR 92/min, creatinine 0.9 mg/dL; no history of heart failure. Which order should the nurse anticipate NEXT?

  1. Oral glycerol 1 mL/kg mixed with cold water to draw fluid out of the eye
  2. IV mannitol after checking renal and cardiac status, then laser iridotomy
  3. A second 500 mg IV acetazolamide dose now and a pressure recheck in 4 hours
  4. Two more doses of pilocarpine 2%, then a recheck of the pressure in 1 hour

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Glycerol is an osmotic option, which makes it tempting, but it is an oral drink and she is actively vomiting, so the IV agent is the dependable choice.
  • B. Correct. A hyperosmotic agent draws fluid out of the eye when drops and acetazolamide have failed; her normal creatinine and lack of heart failure make IV mannitol reasonable.
  • C. Acetazolamide is already acting; the usual follow-on is a lower dose every 6 hours, and repeating the loading dose adds side effects without the rapid drop an osmotic agent gives.
  • D. More miotic seems logical when the angle is still closed, but miotics rarely work while the pressure stays above about 40 to 50 mm Hg, and an hour of delay costs optic nerve.

Community Eye Health's emergency protocol advises a hyperosmotic agent, such as glycerol, isosorbide or mannitol, when the pressure remains dangerously high after the second pilocarpine dose, with IV mannitol 1 to 2 g/kg and extreme caution in cardiovascular disease and renal impairment because the fluid shift expands blood volume and causes a large diuresis. The nurse therefore confirms renal function and heart-failure history, watches blood pressure, lung sounds and urine output, and offers a urinal or bedpan. Merck explains that laser peripheral iridotomy is the definitive treatment once the cornea clears, usually within hours to 1 to 2 days, and that both eyes are treated because the fellow eye has about an 80% risk.

Sources: Murray, Emergency Management: Angle-Closure Glaucoma (2018); Merck Manual Professional: Angle-Closure Glaucoma (2025)

Question 8

A 36-year-old was struck in the left eye by a tree branch while clearing brush. Vision in that eye is reduced to hand motion, the pupil is peaked toward the 9 o'clock position, and a dense ring of subconjunctival hemorrhage surrounds the cornea. Which action should the nurse take FIRST?

  1. Irrigate the eye with normal saline to wash out bark and debris
  2. Tape a rigid shield over the eye so it rests on the orbital bones
  3. Measure the intraocular pressure with a handheld tonometer
  4. Apply a firm pressure patch to keep the eyelid closed

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Irrigation suits chemical injuries and surface debris, but fluid is not directed into a suspected open globe.
  • B. Correct. A peaked pupil and circumferential subconjunctival hemorrhage suggest an open globe; a rigid shield protects it without pressing on the eye.
  • C. A low pressure would support the diagnosis, which makes this tempting, but tonometry presses on the globe and is deferred until rupture is excluded.
  • D. Patching is used for some surface injuries, but pressure on an open globe can push intraocular contents out through the wound.

Signs of an open globe include a peaked or irregular pupil, an abnormal anterior chamber depth, prolapsed dark uveal tissue, markedly reduced vision and dense circumferential subconjunctival hemorrhage, although a posterior rupture can look subtle. Once it is suspected, the eye is protected rather than examined: a rigid shield rests on the bony orbit with no gauze or patch beneath it, and there is no tonometry, palpation, forced lid opening or irrigation. The patient is kept NPO with IV access and the head raised about 30 degrees, receives analgesia, antiemetics, tetanus prophylaxis and systemic antibiotics, and has thin-section CT while ophthalmology is called for repair, generally within 24 hours.

Sources: AAO EyeWiki; ENA TNCC Provider Manual, 9th ed.; Sheehy's Manual of Emergency Care, 8th ed.

Question 9

A 45-year-old with recurrent cold sores has a red, painful left eye with blurred vision and no history of injury. Fluorescein staining shows a branching, tree-like lesion with small knobs at the ends of its branches. Which condition does this pattern suggest?

  1. Pseudomonas keratitis from contact lens overwear
  2. Acute angle-closure glaucoma with corneal edema
  3. Retained particle under the upper eyelid
  4. Herpes simplex keratitis

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. Pseudomonas keratitis, usually after overnight lens wear, produces a dense white stromal infiltrate with purulent discharge that can progress rapidly, not a fine branching epithelial lesion.
  • B. Angle closure causes a steamy, hazy cornea with a fixed mid-dilated pupil, halos and vomiting; it does not produce a branching staining pattern.
  • C. A particle under the lid leaves vertical linear streaks on the upper cornea, and there would usually be a history of debris entering the eye.
  • D. Correct. Dendritic ulcers with terminal buds are the hallmark of epithelial herpes simplex keratitis, which recurs from latent virus and needs prompt antiviral treatment and ophthalmology follow-up.

Herpes simplex virus stays latent after the first infection and can reactivate in the cornea. Epithelial disease appears as a linear branching (dendritic) ulcer whose branches end in terminal bulbs that take up fluorescein. Treatment is a topical antiviral such as ganciclovir gel or trifluridine drops, or an oral antiviral, with immediate ophthalmology referral. Steroid drops are not started from the emergency department, because inappropriate topical corticosteroids can turn a dendrite into a larger geographic ulcer, and repeated attacks scar the cornea and reduce corneal sensation.

Sources: StatPearls (NCBI Bookshelf); Sheehy's Manual of Emergency Care, 8th ed.; Sheehy's Emergency Nursing, 7th ed.

Question 10

A 27-year-old whose jaw locked open during a yawn 1 hour ago is waiting at triage. A colleague suggests he return to the oral surgery clinic in the morning, because his airway is clear and his vital signs are normal. Which reason BEST supports reducing the jaw in the emergency department now?

  1. An open jaw lets the tongue fall back and block the airway within hours
  2. After 24 hours a pseudojoint forms and surgery becomes the usual treatment
  3. Spasm worsens with time, so reduction gets harder and recurrence likelier
  4. The displaced condyle presses on the facial nerve and can cause a lasting palsy

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Drooling and trouble swallowing make airway worry tempting, but an awake patient with an anterior dislocation keeps his airway; the reason to act is the joint itself.
  • B. A pseudojoint and the need for surgery belong to chronic protracted dislocation, which begins after about 2 weeks, not after 1 day.
  • C. Correct. The longer the mandible stays dislocated, the harder it is to reduce and the more likely it is to dislocate again, because the closing muscles spasm and hold the condyle forward.
  • D. Facial nerve injury is described with superior dislocation from a blow to a partly open mouth, which makes this sound plausible, but it is not the risk of an ordinary anterior dislocation from a yawn.

An anterior dislocation leaves the condyle in front of the articular eminence, and spasm of the masseter, temporalis and pterygoid muscles keeps it there. Merck notes that the longer the jaw stays out, the harder it is to reduce and the more likely it is to recur, so prompt reduction is the goal even when the airway is safe. An acute dislocation, presenting within about 2 weeks, usually reduces with the manual (Hippocratic) maneuver. After about 2 weeks muscle shortening makes it a chronic protracted dislocation that often needs general anesthesia, traction or surgery, and a pseudojoint may form. Dislocations present for more than a few hours may also need local anesthetic or sedation to relax the muscles.

Sources: Merck Manual Professional: Mandibular Dislocation (2026); Akinbami, TMJ Dislocation: Systematic Review (2011)
