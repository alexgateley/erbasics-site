Question 1

A 58-year-old with long-standing type 1 diabetes and good insulin adherence presents in DKA without an obvious cause. Which evaluation is MOST important to identify a precipitant?

  1. Hemoglobin A1c to confirm the diabetes diagnosis
  2. A 12-lead ECG, troponin, and infection workup
  3. Serum lipase, since any rise confirms pancreatitis
  4. A urine drug screen as the only needed test

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. A1c shows chronic control but does not identify an acute trigger.
  • B. Correct. Infection and silent myocardial ischemia are common, dangerous triggers, especially in older patients with diabetes.
  • C. Lipase is often elevated nonspecifically in DKA. Pancreatitis requires clinical and imaging correlation.
  • D. Drug use (cocaine, cannabis) can precipitate DKA, but a single test does not rule out infection or ischemia.

Identifying the trigger is essential. The “5 I’s” are Infection (most common), Ischemia or Infarction (MI, stroke, mesenteric ischemia), Inflammation (pancreatitis), Intoxication or drugs (cocaine, SGLT2 inhibitors, steroids, antipsychotics), and Insulin deficiency (missed doses, pump failure, new-onset diabetes). Diabetic patients often have silent MI, so an ECG and troponin are routine. Cultures and imaging are directed by findings.

Sources: AACN Core Curriculum, 8th ed.; ADA Hyperglycemic Crises Consensus (2024); Banasik, Pathophysiology, 7th ed.

Question 2

A patient with opioid use disorder maintained on buprenorphine at home is admitted with cellulitis and severe pain. Which plan is MOST appropriate?

  1. Continue buprenorphine and add multimodal analgesia
  2. Treat the pain with acetaminophen only, given the substance use disorder
  3. Give naloxone to clear the buprenorphine, then start morphine
  4. Stop the buprenorphine so that full-agonist opioids can take effect

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. Current guidance is to continue buprenorphine during acute pain and add multimodal therapy, with higher-dose full agonists if pain is uncontrolled.
  • B. Undertreating pain in patients with opioid use disorder is stigmatizing and increases relapse risk.
  • C. Naloxone precipitates severe withdrawal and is dangerous outside of overdose.
  • D. Abruptly stopping buprenorphine causes withdrawal and relapse risk; continuation is recommended.

Buprenorphine is a partial agonist with high receptor affinity. Continuing it maintains stability, and acute pain is managed with scheduled acetaminophen, NSAIDs, regional anesthesia, and full agonists at higher doses if needed. Splitting the buprenorphine dose to every 6 to 8 hours adds analgesia. Non-judgmental care and coordination with the prescribing program are essential.

Sources: Elisha et al., Nurse Anesthesia, 7th ed.; AACN Core Curriculum, 8th ed.

Question 3

Ten minutes after starting a unit of PRBCs, a patient reports back pain and a feeling of doom. Temperature rises to 39.2°C, BP falls to 84/46 mm Hg, and the urine in the Foley bag turns red-brown. Which reaction is MOST likely, and what is its mechanism?

  1. Febrile nonhemolytic: antibodies to donor white cells
  2. Allergic: IgE reaction to donor plasma proteins
  3. Acute hemolytic: ABO-incompatible cells are destroyed
  4. TRALI: donor antibodies injuring the lungs

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. A febrile reaction causes fever and chills without hypotension or hemoglobinuria.
  • B. Allergic reactions cause urticaria and pruritus and, when severe, anaphylaxis without hemoglobinuria.
  • C. Correct. Recipient antibodies lyse donor red cells, causing fever, hypotension, pain, and hemoglobinuria.
  • D. TRALI causes hypoxemia and bilateral infiltrates, not hemoglobinuria with back pain.

Acute hemolytic transfusion reactions, usually from ABO incompatibility caused by identification errors, cause complement-mediated intravascular hemolysis. Signs include fever, chills, back or flank pain, a sense of doom, hypotension, hemoglobinuria, DIC, and AKI. The nurse stops the transfusion immediately, keeps the IV open with saline through new tubing, notifies the provider and blood bank, returns the unit and samples, supports BP and urine output, and monitors for DIC. Prevention depends on rigorous two-identifier verification at the bedside.

Sources: INS Infusion Therapy Standards, 9th ed.; AACN Core Curriculum, 8th ed.

Question 4

A trauma patient in hemorrhagic shock triggers the massive transfusion protocol. Which resuscitation approach is MOST appropriate?

  1. Give balanced product ratios guided by TEG or ROTEM
  2. Give RBCs first, then plasma once the INR is above 1.5
  3. Give 2 L of crystalloid first, then start blood products
  4. Target a Hgb above 10 before giving plasma or platelets

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. Balanced 1:1:1 (or 1:1:2) resuscitation plus TEG or ROTEM guidance limits coagulopathy while hemorrhage is controlled.
  • B. Waiting for lab abnormalities leads to dilutional coagulopathy. Plasma should be given early with the RBCs.
  • C. Large crystalloid volumes worsen dilution, acidosis, and hypothermia and should be minimized.
  • D. MTP is guided by hemostasis and hemodynamics, not Hgb targets, and products are given together.

Damage-control resuscitation includes early balanced blood products (the PROPPR trial showed less death from exsanguination at 24 hours with 1:1:1), minimal crystalloid, permissive hypotension until bleeding is controlled (except with TBI), tranexamic acid within 3 hours, prevention of the lethal triad (hypothermia, acidosis, coagulopathy), and calcium replacement. Endpoints are hemodynamic stability, control of hemorrhage, and correction of coagulopathy on viscoelastic testing.

Sources: McQuillan, Trauma Nursing, 5th ed.; ACS TQIP Massive Transfusion Guide (2014); European Trauma Bleeding Guideline (2023)

Question 5

An 82-year-old with heart failure receives 3 units of PRBCs over 4 hours. The patient develops dyspnea, BP 182/96 mm Hg, JVD, bibasilar crackles, and an elevated BNP. Which intervention is MOST appropriate?

  1. IV furosemide and upright positioning
  2. Supportive care with oxygen; avoid diuretics
  3. IV methylprednisolone and epinephrine
  4. Broad-spectrum antibiotics and cultures

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. Hypertension, JVD, crackles, and a high BNP indicate TACO, which is treated with diuresis and oxygen.
  • B. Oxygen helps, but withholding diuretics is the approach for TRALI. Volume overload needs diuresis.
  • C. These treat anaphylaxis, which is not suggested by hypertension and volume overload.
  • D. Nothing suggests a septic transfusion reaction.

Transfusion-associated circulatory overload is hydrostatic pulmonary edema from volume excess. It is now one of the leading causes of transfusion-related death. Risk factors include age over 70, heart failure, renal failure, positive fluid balance, and rapid infusion. TACO features hypertension, JVD, elevated BNP, and a response to diuretics. TRALI features hypotension, fever, normal filling pressures, and no response to diuretics. Prevention includes transfusing one unit at a time, slower rates (about 1 mL/kg/h), and diuretics between units for at-risk patients.

Sources: AACN Core Curriculum, 8th ed.; Goldman-Cecil Medicine, 27th ed.

Question 6

A patient on long-term opioids for cancer pain becomes oversedated after an increase in hydromorphone. The patient is arousable only to sternal rub, with RR 7 and SpO₂ 88%. Which naloxone approach is MOST appropriate?

  1. Hold naloxone because the patient needs the opioids for pain
  2. Dilute naloxone; titrate small doses to breathing
  3. Flumazenil instead of naloxone to preserve analgesia
  4. Naloxone 2 mg IV push to reverse fully and quickly

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Life-threatening respiratory depression must be treated.
  • B. Correct. Small titrated doses restore adequate ventilation while preserving analgesia and avoiding withdrawal.
  • C. Flumazenil does not reverse opioids.
  • D. Full reversal in an opioid-dependent patient causes abrupt withdrawal, severe pain, and possible pulmonary edema or arrhythmias.

For opioid-induced respiratory depression in opioid-tolerant patients, naloxone is diluted (for example, 0.4 mg in 10 mL) and given in small increments (0.04–0.08 mg every 2–3 minutes) to restore adequate respirations, not full alertness. The nurse supports ventilation with a bag-mask if needed, monitors for re-sedation (naloxone lasts a shorter time than many opioids), reassesses the opioid regimen, and uses capnography for high-risk patients.

Sources: Ferrell, Oxford Palliative Nursing, 5th ed.; AACN Core Curriculum, 8th ed.

Question 7

A patient develops hypotension, diffuse urticaria, and wheezing after an IV antibiotic. Epinephrine is given IM. Which statement BEST explains why epinephrine is the first-line treatment?

  1. It combines α₁, β₁, and β₂ receptor effects
  2. Its β₁ effect alone restores cardiac output
  3. Its central α₂ effect lowers sympathetic tone
  4. Its H₁ receptor blockade stops histamine effects

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. α₁ reverses vasodilation and edema, β₁ supports cardiac output, and β₂ bronchodilates and reduces mediator release.
  • B. β₁ effects alone would not reverse vasodilation, airway edema, or bronchospasm.
  • C. Epinephrine’s benefit comes from its peripheral α₁, β₁, and β₂ actions.
  • D. Epinephrine does not block histamine receptors. That is how diphenhydramine works, and it is an adjunct only.

Epinephrine stimulates α₁, β₁, and β₂ receptors. At low doses, β effects predominate. At higher doses, α₁ vasoconstriction dominates. In anaphylaxis it reverses vasodilation and capillary leak (α₁), supports contractility and heart rate (β₁), dilates the bronchi, and stabilizes mast cells (β₂). IM epinephrine (0.3–0.5 mg of 1 mg/mL into the thigh) is given first, and antihistamines and steroids are adjuncts.

Sources: Urden, Critical Care Nursing, 10th ed.; Mosby's 2026 Nursing Drug Reference

Question 8

A 24-year-old with type 1 diabetes has deep, rapid respirations. Arterial blood gas on room air: pH 7.18, PaCO₂ 22 mm Hg, HCO₃⁻ 8 mEq/L, PaO₂ 104 mm Hg. Sodium is 136 and chloride 100 mEq/L. Which interpretation is correct?

  1. High anion gap acidosis with appropriate compensation
  2. Normal anion gap metabolic acidosis from bicarbonate loss
  3. Metabolic acidosis with a superimposed respiratory acidosis
  4. Acute respiratory alkalosis with a normal anion gap

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. This is a high anion gap metabolic acidosis with appropriate respiratory compensation. The anion gap is 136 − (100 + 8) = 28. Winters formula predicts a PaCO₂ of 1.5 × 8 + 8 ± 2, or 18 to 22 mm Hg, so the measured 22 is the expected compensation.
  • B. The gap of 28 is well above the normal 8 to 12 mEq/L.
  • C. That would require a PaCO₂ above the predicted range of 18 to 22 mm Hg.
  • D. The pH is acidemic, so the low PaCO₂ is compensation and not the primary disorder.

Metabolic acidosis: calculate the anion gap (sodium minus the sum of chloride and bicarbonate, normally 8 to 12). A high gap points to ketoacidosis, lactic acidosis, renal failure or a toxic ingestion; a normal gap to diarrhea or renal tubular acidosis. Winters formula checks compensation. The hyperventilation is compensatory, so if such a patient is intubated the minute ventilation must stay high.

Sources: Wilkins' Clinical Assessment in RC, 10th ed.; ADA Hyperglycemic Crises Consensus (2024)

Question 9

A patient with anaphylaxis after IV contrast has received two doses of IM epinephrine 0.5 mg and 2 L of crystalloid, but remains hypotensive (BP 74/40) with wheezing. Which intervention is MOST appropriate next?

  1. Dopamine infusion at 5 mcg/kg/min
  2. IV diphenhydramine and methylprednisolone only
  3. A titrated IV epinephrine infusion
  4. Epinephrine 1 mg IV push, the arrest dose

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Dopamine lacks epinephrine’s combined α₁ and β₂ benefits for anaphylaxis.
  • B. Antihistamines and steroids are adjuncts. They do not reverse shock or bronchospasm acutely.
  • C. Correct. Refractory anaphylaxis is treated with a continuous epinephrine infusion, titrated to BP and bronchospasm, with monitoring.
  • D. Arrest-dose IV epinephrine in a patient with a pulse can cause severe hypertension, arrhythmias, and myocardial ischemia.

Epinephrine’s α₁ effects reverse vasodilation and capillary leak, and its β₂ effects dilate the bronchi and stabilize mast cells, which is why it is first-line for anaphylaxis. When shock persists after repeated IM doses and fluids, a titrated IV infusion (for example, 0.05–0.1 mcg/kg/min, or 1–10 mcg/min) is recommended. Patients on β-blockers may need glucagon. Airway edema calls for early preparation for a difficult airway.

Sources: Urden, Critical Care Nursing, 10th ed.; AACN Core Curriculum, 8th ed.

Question 10

A patient taking metoprolol develops anaphylaxis after IV contrast. After two doses of IM epinephrine, 2 L of crystalloid, and supplemental oxygen, BP remains 74/40 mm Hg and wheezing persists. Which medication is MOST specifically indicated now?

  1. Nebulized albuterol as the main therapy
  2. Diphenhydramine 50 mg IV as the main therapy
  3. Glucagon 1–5 mg IV over 5 minutes
  4. Methylprednisolone 125 mg IV as the main therapy

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Albuterol may help wheezing but does nothing for the circulatory collapse.
  • B. Antihistamines are adjuncts and do not reverse shock or bronchospasm.
  • C. Correct. Glucagon raises cAMP independent of β-receptors and can reverse epinephrine-resistant anaphylaxis in patients on β-blockers.
  • D. Steroids act over hours and are not a rescue therapy for shock.

IM epinephrine (0.3–0.5 mg in the anterolateral thigh, repeated every 5–15 minutes) is first-line for anaphylaxis, followed by an epinephrine infusion if the patient does not respond. β-blockers blunt the response to epinephrine and can cause refractory anaphylaxis. Glucagon bypasses β-receptors to increase contractility and heart rate. Aggressive fluids and airway management are essential. Antihistamines and steroids are adjuncts only.

Sources: AACN Core Curriculum, 8th ed.; Mosby's 2026 Nursing Drug Reference
