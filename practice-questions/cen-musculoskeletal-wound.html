Question 1

A 40-year-old gardener stepped on a rusty nail that went through the sole of his shoe into his foot. He completed the childhood tetanus series and received Tdap 7 years ago. The puncture has been cleaned. Which tetanus prophylaxis should the nurse anticipate?

  1. A Td or Tdap booster now together with tetanus immune globulin
  2. No booster, since his last dose was given within the past 10 years
  3. A Td or Tdap booster today, without tetanus immune globulin
  4. Tetanus immune globulin today, then a booster at the follow-up visit

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Immune globulin is added for dirty wounds when the person has fewer than three doses, an unknown history, HIV or severe immunodeficiency, none of which applies.
  • B. The 10-year interval applies to clean, minor wounds; a soil-contaminated puncture uses the shorter 5-year interval.
  • C. Correct. A puncture wound is a dirty wound, and with three or more prior doses a booster is due once 5 years have passed; immune globulin is not needed.
  • D. Immune globulin gives short-lived passive protection to people without a reliable vaccine history; a fully immunized adult needs the booster itself, today.

Tetanus prophylaxis depends on two questions: what kind of wound it is, and how many doses the patient has had and how recently. Puncture wounds and wounds contaminated with dirt, soil, feces or saliva, along with burns, crush injuries and frostbite, count as dirty or major wounds. A person with a complete primary series needs a booster for a dirty wound if 5 or more years have passed since the last dose, but for a clean minor wound only after 10 years. Td or Tdap may be used for the booster. Antibiotics are not given to prevent tetanus; the wound is watched for infection instead.

Sources: CDC Tetanus Clinical Guidance and Pink Book; Sheehy's Manual of Emergency Care, 8th ed.; ENA Core Curriculum, 8th ed.

Question 2

A 72-year-old with Clostridioides difficile diarrhea has been boarding in the ED for 20 hours and has had frequent liquid stools. The skin around the anus and across both inner buttocks is diffusely red and shiny, with patchy, superficial erosions and irregular edges. The skin over the sacrum and ischial tuberosities is intact. Which plan should the nurse implement?

  1. Wash with soap and warm water and rub the skin dry after each stool
  2. Fit an adult diaper snugly to hold the stool away from the skin
  3. Document a stage 2 pressure injury and apply a hydrocolloid dressing
  4. Gentle pH-balanced cleansing after each stool, then a barrier product

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. Cleaning after each stool is right, but soap and rubbing are among the practices the review advises against because they strip and abrade already damaged skin.
  • B. Containing stool sounds protective, but a snug diaper traps moisture against the skin; absorbent pads are preferred because they let air flow.
  • C. Shallow open areas look like stage 2, but these follow the skin exposed to stool rather than a bony prominence; Merck excludes maceration and excoriation from stage 2, NPIAP names moisture damage as a stage 2 look-alike, and a dressing seals liquid stool against the skin.
  • D. Correct. This is incontinence-associated dermatitis, and the evidence supports gentle cleansing after each episode with a cleanser of pH 4.0 to 6.8, blotting rather than rubbing, and a zinc oxide or film barrier.

Incontinence-associated dermatitis is a form of moisture-associated skin damage caused by chemical irritants in urine and stool, not by pressure. Banharak and colleagues note that staff who confuse it with pressure injury apply the wrong treatment. Clues here are the diffuse redness and erosions in the perianal skin and folds while the skin over bone is intact. Care is a structured skin regimen: cleanse after every episode with a pH-balanced cleanser, pat dry, apply a barrier such as zinc oxide or a spray film, prefer pads to diapers and position on the side. Turning still matters, because pressure risk remains high in a patient lying in stool.

Sources: Banharak et al., J Multidiscip Healthc (2021); Edsberg et al., Revised NPIAP Pressure Injury Staging System (2016); Merck Manual Professional: Pressure Injuries (2026)

Question 3

A 30-year-old fell onto an outstretched hand while skateboarding. The left elbow is held flexed at about 45 degrees with a prominent olecranon, and radiographs confirm a posterior elbow dislocation; the team is preparing for reduction. Which finding should the nurse report to the provider IMMEDIATELY?

  1. Swelling that blurs the bony landmarks of the elbow
  2. A cool, pale hand with a weak radial pulse
  3. Pain rated 9 out of 10 when the elbow is moved
  4. A forearm that looks shorter than the right forearm

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Swelling around a dislocated elbow is expected and often hides the landmarks; it is monitored but does not by itself signal a threatened limb.
  • B. Correct. A cool, pale hand with a weak pulse suggests brachial artery compromise, which turns the reduction into a limb-saving emergency and may need vascular evaluation.
  • C. Severe pain with movement is typical of a dislocated joint and is treated with analgesia and sedation for the reduction, not reported as a new emergency.
  • D. A shortened forearm with a prominent olecranon is the classic appearance of a posterior elbow dislocation, so it confirms the diagnosis rather than a complication.

Most elbow dislocations are posterior and follow a fall on an extended arm. The elbow is held flexed at about 45 degrees, the olecranon sits prominently behind the epicondyles, the forearm looks short, and swelling can hide these landmarks, so those findings are expected. The dangerous associations are fractures, ulnar or median nerve injury and brachial artery injury. Any injured limb is checked at once for neurovascular injury distal to it, so the nurse records the pulse, color, temperature, movement and sensation of the hand before and after the reduction, which uses steady traction with the elbow flexed. Post-reduction radiographs look for missed fractures.

Sources: Merck Manual Professional: Overview of Fractures (2025); AAOS OrthoInfo

Question 4

A patient with a stab wound to the upper thigh has a firm, rapidly enlarging swelling around the wound, a palpable thrill, and a weak dorsalis pedis pulse compared with the other side. What do these findings mean?

  1. Expected swelling after a deep stab wound
  2. A fracture that needs a traction splint
  3. Hard signs of arterial injury
  4. A superficial vein injury that can be bandaged

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. An expanding hematoma is never normal.
  • B. Traction on a bleeding arterial injury is contraindicated.
  • C. Correct. An expanding hematoma, thrill and pulse deficit are hard signs that mandate vascular surgery; bleeding is controlled with pressure or a tourniquet and the wound is not probed.
  • D. A thrill and pulse deficit mean arterial flow into the tissue.

Hard signs of vascular injury are pulsatile bleeding, an expanding or pulsatile hematoma, a palpable thrill or audible bruit, an absent or diminished distal pulse, and signs of limb ischemia; any one mandates surgical evaluation. The paramedic controls hemorrhage with pressure, a hemostatic dressing or a tourniquet, marks the pulse sites, avoids probing or removing clots, and transports rapidly to a trauma center.

Sources: NAEMT PHTLS, 10th ed.; ACS/ACEP/NAEMSP Hemorrhage Control (2023); ACS ATLS, 11th ed.

Question 5

A 27-year-old motorcyclist has a grossly unstable right knee after a crash. Paramedics report the knee looked deformed at the scene, but it is aligned on arrival. Data are: right dorsalis pedis pulse palpable but weaker than the left; capillary refill 3 seconds on the right; ankle-brachial index 0.82 on the right and 1.05 on the left; weak right ankle dorsiflexion. Which intervention should the nurse anticipate NEXT?

  1. Repeat ankle-brachial index in 24 hours to see whether it trends down
  2. A knee immobilizer and outpatient MRI, since a pulse is palpable
  3. MRI of the knee to grade the ligament injuries before vascular studies
  4. CT angiography of the right leg and vascular surgery consultation

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. Serial checks are part of care, but an abnormal index already needs imaging; delaying revascularization past about 8 hours sharply raises amputation rates.
  • B. A palpable pulse is reassuring at first glance, but it does not exclude an intimal tear that can progress to occlusion.
  • C. Ligament imaging guides later reconstruction, but a threatened limb comes first, and MRI does not assess the artery.
  • D. Correct. An ABI below 0.9 after a knee dislocation strongly suggests popliteal artery injury and calls for urgent vascular imaging and a surgeon.

A knee that was deformed at the scene and is now aligned and grossly unstable should be treated as a spontaneously reduced knee dislocation, which is often missed. Arterial injury occurs in about 18% of these injuries, and palpable distal pulses do not reliably rule it out. An ankle-brachial index below 0.9 strongly suggests arterial compromise and leads to urgent CT angiography, now the imaging test of choice, with early vascular surgery input, because amputation rates climb when revascularization is delayed beyond about 8 hours. The weak dorsiflexion suggests a peroneal nerve injury, seen in roughly 10% to 40%, which the nurse documents along with serial pulse checks.

Sources: ENA TNCC Provider Manual, 9th ed.; ACS ATLS, 11th ed.; Sheehy's Manual of Emergency Care, 8th ed.

Question 6

MRI confirms a large L5-S1 disc herniation compressing the cauda equina in a 52-year-old whose painless urinary retention began 10 hours ago. A bladder scan showed 820 mL before a catheter was placed. Which plan should the nurse anticipate?

  1. An epidural steroid injection to reduce nerve root inflammation
  2. Admission for bed rest, analgesia and an indwelling catheter
  3. High-dose IV dexamethasone and a repeat MRI in 48 hours
  4. Urgent surgical decompression of the compressed nerve roots

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. Epidural steroid injections are used for persistent radicular pain from a disc herniation, but they do not decompress the cauda equina and have no role once bladder function is lost.
  • B. The catheter relieves retention and analgesia is needed, so this plan treats the symptoms, but it leaves the nerve roots compressed, and conservative care is not appropriate once cauda equina syndrome is confirmed.
  • C. Dexamethasone is standard for spinal cord compression from metastatic cancer, which makes it tempting, but steroids have no established routine role in disc-related cauda equina syndrome and do not relieve a mechanical compression, and waiting 48 hours risks permanent bladder and bowel loss.
  • D. Correct. Cauda equina syndrome from a disc herniation is a surgical emergency; decompression within 48 hours of onset is associated with better bladder, motor, sensory and sexual outcomes, so surgery should not wait once MRI confirms compression.

Cauda equina syndrome is often divided by bladder function. In the incomplete form, bladder sensation and some voluntary control are preserved although voiding is altered; in the retention form, there is painless urinary retention with loss of bladder sensation. Massive central lumbar disc herniation, most often at L4-L5 or L5-S1, causes about half of cases. Decompression within 48 hours of onset is associated with better bladder, motor, sensory and sexual outcomes, and some studies show better bladder recovery when the incomplete form is decompressed within 24 hours. Retention-stage disease carries a poorer prognosis, but decompression remains urgent to limit further loss, and corticosteroids have no established routine role.

Sources: Sheehy's Manual of Emergency Care, 8th ed.; ENA Core Curriculum, 8th ed.

Question 7

The nurse is teaching a client to walk with crutches using a three-point gait after a right ankle fracture. Which instruction is correct?

  1. “Move the right crutch with the left leg, then the left crutch with the right leg.”
  2. “Step up with the right leg first, then bring the crutches and left leg.”
  3. “Rest your weight on the armpit pads when your hands get tired.”
  4. “Move both crutches and the right leg forward, then the left leg.”

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. This is the two-point gait, used when both legs can bear partial weight.
  • B. The unaffected left leg goes up first (“up with the good”), then the crutches and the injured leg.
  • C. Pressure on the pads compresses nerves in the axilla, most often the radial nerve (crutch palsy); weight goes through the hands.
  • D. Correct. In the three-point gait the injured leg and both crutches advance together while the unaffected leg bears the weight.

Crutch teaching: pads two to three fingerbreadths below the axilla, elbows flexed 15 to 30 degrees, weight on the hands; leaning on the pads risks radial nerve injury. Three-point gait for one non-weight-bearing leg; two-point and four-point when both legs can bear some weight. Stairs: good leg up first, crutches and bad leg down first.

Bottom line: In the three-point crutch gait, both crutches and the injured leg advance together, then the unaffected leg steps through and bears the weight.

Sources: Ignatavicius, Med-Surg Nursing, 11th ed.; Potter & Perry Fundamentals, 12th ed.; Merck Manual: How To Fit and Walk With Underarm Crutches

Question 8

A volleyball player fell on an outstretched arm. The acromion is prominent, the arm is held slightly away from the body, and he will not move it. Which neurologic finding should the paramedic check and record before and after splinting?

  1. Sensation over the tip of the little finger
  2. Strength of wrist extension against resistance
  3. Strength of thumb opposition against the fingertips
  4. Sensation over the lateral shoulder

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. Tempting as a routine hand check, but this tests the ulnar nerve, which is not the classic deficit here.
  • B. This tests the radial nerve, which is more often linked with humeral shaft injury.
  • C. This tests the median nerve, which is not the nerve classically at risk.
  • D. Correct. Reduced sensation over the deltoid suggests axillary nerve injury, the classic deficit with this dislocation.

An anterior shoulder dislocation displaces the humeral head forward and down, leaving a prominent acromion, with the elbow held slightly out from the side and little willingness to move. Axillary nerve injury can cause motor and sensory deficits, and decreased sensation over the deltoid region suggests it. The second step is matching the nerve to the injury. Pulses, capillary refill and distal movement are also recorded, because the guideline asks for neurovascular reassessment after any manipulation or splinting. Analgesia comes before moving the arm, and reduction is done in hospital.

Sources: NASEMSO Model EMS Guidelines v3.0 (2022); Merck Manual Professional: Shoulder Dislocations (2025)

Question 9

A 62-year-old is being discharged on oral cephalexin for nonpurulent cellulitis of the lower leg. The nurse notes cracked, macerated skin between the toes of the same foot. Which instruction BEST supports healing and lowers the risk of recurrence?

  1. Keep the leg raised and treat the skin between the toes
  2. Cover the toe spaces with an occlusive dressing to keep them moist
  3. Keep the leg lowered below the heart to improve blood flow to the infection
  4. Stop the antibiotic when the redness fades

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. Elevation lets edema and inflammatory fluid drain, and treating fissured or macerated toe webs (often tinea pedis) removes the portal of entry that allows streptococci to reinfect the leg.
  • B. Moist dressings help many wounds heal, but trapping moisture between the toes worsens maceration and fungal growth; the toe webs need to be kept clean and dry and the underlying tinea treated.
  • C. Dependency can seem to help by delivering more blood and antibiotic to the area, but it worsens edema, which slows healing and is itself a risk factor for cellulitis; elevation is the recommended position.
  • D. Shorter courses do limit resistance, and 5 days is often enough, but redness can fade before the infection has cleared; patients finish the prescribed course and return if it is not improving.

The IDSA guideline recommends elevating the affected area and treating predisposing factors in cellulitis. For lower-extremity infection, the interdigital toe spaces should be examined carefully, because treating fissuring, scaling or maceration (frequently tinea pedis) can clear the colonizing streptococci and reduce recurrence. Edema, obesity, venous insufficiency and eczema are other predisposing conditions. Nonpurulent cellulitis without systemic signs is treated as an outpatient with an antistreptococcal oral agent such as cephalexin for about 5 days, extended if the infection has not improved.

Sources: IDSA Skin and Soft Tissue Infections (2014); Sheehy's Manual of Emergency Care, 8th ed.; Lewis's Medical-Surgical Nursing, 13th ed.

Question 10

A frail 88-year-old woman with dementia, incontinence and a hip fracture is boarding in the ED and cannot reposition herself. Data are: serum albumin 2.8 g/dL, hemoglobin 9.6 g/dL, history of type 2 diabetes, and she ate about a third of her breakfast. The nurse completes a Braden assessment. Which finding is scored directly on the Braden scale?

  1. Skin often damp from incontinence
  2. Her history of type 2 diabetes
  3. Her age of 88 years and low hemoglobin
  4. Her serum albumin of 2.8 g/dL

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. Moisture is one of the six Braden subscales, scored by how often the skin is exposed to wetness.
  • B. Diabetes affects perfusion and healing, which is why it tempts, but no Braden subscale scores diagnoses.
  • C. Advanced age and anemia raise risk, but neither appears among the Braden subscales; they are considered alongside the score.
  • D. Low albumin is often linked to pressure injury risk, but the Braden nutrition subscale scores the patient's usual food intake, not a laboratory value.

The Braden scale scores six subscales: sensory perception, moisture, activity, mobility, nutrition and friction/shear. Lower totals mean higher risk, and each low subscale points to a specific prevention step, which is why the subscales matter more than the total. For this patient, frequent moisture from incontinence calls for prompt cleansing and a barrier product, immobility for scheduled turning, and poor intake for a nutrition referral. Laboratory values, age and diagnoses such as diabetes are real risk factors, but the nurse weighs them in clinical judgment because the tool does not score them.

Sources: Merck Manual Professional: Pressure Injuries (2026)
