Question 1

A 3-year-old (14 kg) drank from a bottle of alcohol-containing mouthwash 2 hours ago. On arrival he was sleepy with a glucose of 42 mg/dL, and IV dextrose was given as ordered. Thirty minutes later he is awake and talking but still sways when he stands. Data are: HR 118, RR 22, SpO₂ 98% on room air, point-of-care glucose 104 mg/dL. His parents ask to take him home now that his sugar is normal. Which plan should the nurse anticipate?

  1. Give activated charcoal by mouth now that he is awake enough to drink
  2. Anticipate fomepizole to shorten the time he stays intoxicated
  3. Observe with repeated glucose checks until he is steady and clinically sober
  4. Discharge him if a repeat glucose in 1 hour is still above 70 mg/dL

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Ethanol is absorbed rapidly, with peak levels within 30 to 90 minutes, so 2 hours after ingestion there is little left to remove, and charcoal adds vomiting and aspiration risk in a child who was drowsy.
  • B. Fomepizole blocks alcohol dehydrogenase and is given for methanol or ethylene glycol poisoning; it has no role in isolated ethanol toxicity and would slow, not speed, ethanol clearance.
  • C. Correct. Ethanol keeps suppressing gluconeogenesis until it is cleared, so hypoglycemia can recur after a dextrose bolus. He stays under observation, with glucose rechecks and airway monitoring, until he is clinically sober and his metabolic state is stable.
  • D. Tempting because one more normal reading seems reassuring, but he is still ataxic, so ethanol is still present and still suppressing glucose production. A single recheck does not cover the hours until he is sober.

The Merck Manual notes that young children who drink alcohol are at significant risk of hypoglycemia because ethanol impairs gluconeogenesis and their smaller glycogen stores are depleted quickly, and a fingerstick glucose is one of the few useful tests in acute intoxication. StatPearls names mouthwashes, hand sanitizers and colognes as non-beverage ethanol sources that mostly poison children, calls for IV dextrose for hypoglycemia with particular attention in children, and advises observation until clinical sobriety is achieved and metabolic abnormalities have resolved; discharge requires a patient who is awake, oriented, ambulatory and clinically sober. Correcting one low glucose does not end the risk: while ethanol remains, glucose production stays suppressed, so the nurse rechecks the glucose often and watches the airway until the child walks steadily.

Sources: Merck Manual, Professional Version; StatPearls (NCBI Bookshelf)

Question 2

A patient taking dabigatran for atrial fibrillation (last dose 4 hours ago) presents with an acute intracerebral hemorrhage and a GCS of 12. Which reversal agent is MOST specific?

  1. Vitamin K 10 mg IV
  2. Andexanet alfa
  3. Protamine sulfate
  4. Idarucizumab

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. Vitamin K reverses warfarin, not direct thrombin inhibitors.
  • B. Andexanet reverses factor Xa inhibitors such as apixaban and rivaroxaban.
  • C. Protamine reverses heparin (fully) and LMWH (partially).
  • D. Correct. Idarucizumab is a monoclonal antibody fragment that binds and reverses dabigatran within minutes.

Anticoagulant reversal is matched to the drug. Warfarin is reversed with 4-factor PCC plus IV vitamin K. Dabigatran is reversed with idarucizumab (or hemodialysis). Factor Xa inhibitors (apixaban, rivaroxaban, edoxaban) are reversed with 4-factor PCC, which the 2026 NCS/SCCM update suggests over andexanet alfa in ICH; andexanet left the US market in December 2025. Heparin is reversed with protamine. In ICH, rapid reversal, BP control (SBP about 140), and neurosurgical evaluation are priorities, and the nurse documents the time of the last anticoagulant dose.

Sources: Elsevier's 2025 Intravenous Medications; AHA/ASA Spontaneous ICH Guideline (2022); NCS/SCCM Antithrombotic Reversal (2016); NCS/SCCM Antithrombotic ICH Update (2026)

Question 3

A patient with acute ischemic stroke symptoms, last known well 90 minutes ago, has a noncontrast CT negative for hemorrhage. BP is 172/98 mm Hg and glucose is 132 mg/dL. Which finding is a CONTRAINDICATION to IV alteplase?

  1. Platelet count of 150,000/mm³
  2. Current warfarin use with an INR of 1.9
  3. An NIHSS score of 4 with disabling aphasia
  4. Age of 84 years (living independently)

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Thrombolysis is contraindicated only with platelets below 100,000/mm³.
  • B. Correct. An INR above 1.7 in a patient on warfarin is a contraindication.
  • C. Mild but disabling deficits, such as aphasia, are appropriate to treat.
  • D. Advanced age alone is not a contraindication within the 3-hour window.

Contraindications to IV thrombolysis include intracranial hemorrhage on CT, significant head trauma or stroke within 3 months, intracranial or spinal surgery within 3 months, active internal bleeding, platelets below 100,000, INR above 1.7 or PT above 15 seconds, therapeutic heparin with elevated aPTT, recent DOAC use (within 48 hours) without normal testing, and BP that cannot be lowered below 185/110. Glucose must be checked because hypoglycemia can mimic stroke.

Sources: AHA/ASA Acute Ischemic Stroke (2026); AHA ACLS Provider Manual (2025)

Question 4

A patient with a severe TBI (GCS 6) has just been intubated in the ED and is awaiting transfer to the ICU. Which intervention MOST effectively prevents secondary brain injury during the first 24 hours?

  1. Avoid hypotension and hypoxemia (SBP, SpO₂ targets)
  2. Use hypotonic fluids to prevent hypernatremia
  3. Give prophylactic hyperventilation to a PaCO₂ of 25–30
  4. Give high-dose steroids to reduce cerebral edema

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. Even single episodes of hypotension or hypoxemia markedly worsen outcomes after TBI.
  • B. Hypotonic fluids worsen cerebral edema.
  • C. Prophylactic hyperventilation reduces cerebral blood flow and worsens ischemia.
  • D. Steroids increase mortality in TBI (CRASH trial).

Secondary brain injury is driven by hypotension, hypoxemia, hypercarbia or hypocarbia, fever, hypoglycemia or hyperglycemia, seizures, and intracranial hypertension. Hypotension (SBP below 90–110 depending on age) and hypoxemia (SpO₂ below 90%) are the strongest predictors of poor outcome. Priorities include maintaining SBP at or above target, SpO₂ of 94% or higher, PaCO₂ of 35–45, normothermia, normoglycemia, seizure prophylaxis for 7 days, the head of the bed at 30°, and early ICP monitoring when indicated.

Sources: BTF Severe TBI Guidelines, 4th ed. (2016); Hickey & Strayer, Neuro Nursing, 8th ed.

Question 5

A 27-year-old who is 20 weeks pregnant shares an apartment with her brother, who was admitted through the ED last night with meningococcal meningitis. She is well and afebrile and has no drug allergies. The provider plans chemoprophylaxis. Which regimen should the nurse anticipate for her?

  1. A meningococcal vaccine alone, so that antibiotics are avoided in pregnancy
  2. Ciprofloxacin 500 mg orally as a single dose
  3. Rifampin 600 mg orally twice daily for 2 days
  4. Ceftriaxone 250 mg IM as a single dose

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. Avoiding drugs in pregnancy sounds protective, but CDC directs antibiotic chemoprophylaxis for close contacts regardless of vaccination status, so a vaccine does not replace it.
  • B. A single oral dose is convenient and widely used for adults, but CDC lists ciprofloxacin as not recommended in pregnancy.
  • C. Rifampin is the classic regimen for household contacts, which is why it tempts, but CDC lists it as not recommended for pregnant women.
  • D. Correct. A single IM dose of ceftriaxone is one of CDC's standard regimens and, unlike the two oral options, is not marked as not recommended in pregnancy.

Household members are close contacts of a patient with meningococcal disease and should receive antibiotic chemoprophylaxis as soon as possible, regardless of vaccination status, because the risk of secondary disease is highest in the first few days; given more than 14 days after the patient's onset it is probably of limited or no benefit. CDC's surveillance manual lists rifampin (10 mg/kg, maximum 600 mg, every 12 hours for 2 days), ceftriaxone (a single 250 mg IM dose for those 15 and older) and ciprofloxacin (a single 500 mg dose) as the standard regimens, and marks both rifampin and ciprofloxacin as not recommended for pregnant women. The ceftriaxone label reports no embryotoxicity or teratogenicity in animal studies, so ceftriaxone is the regimen to anticipate. The nurse also teaches her to seek care at once for fever, headache or rash.

Sources: Ceftriaxone for injection label (Hikma, 2026); CDC Surveillance Manual, Chapter 8: Meningococcal Disease (2024)

Question 6

A patient with a severe traumatic brain injury has an ICP of 28 mm Hg sustained for 5 minutes. MAP is 88 mm Hg, PaCO₂ is 38 mm Hg, and the patient is sedated. The head is turned to the right, and the ETT ties are tight around the neck. Which intervention should the nurse perform FIRST?

  1. Cluster all care into one session so the patient can rest
  2. Start D5W at 100 mL/hr to maintain intravascular volume
  3. Hyperventilate to a PaCO₂ of 25 to keep ICP controlled
  4. Head midline, HOB at 30°, and loosen the ETT ties

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. Doing many stimulating activities back-to-back can raise ICP. Care should be spaced out with ICP monitoring.
  • B. Hypotonic fluids worsen cerebral edema.
  • C. Prolonged hyperventilation causes cerebral vasoconstriction and ischemia. It is a brief rescue measure only.
  • D. Correct. Removing obstruction to jugular venous outflow is a fast, noninvasive first step.

Tiered ICP management begins with basic measures: head midline and neck neutral, HOB at 30°, avoiding tight ETT ties or cervical collars that compress the jugular veins, adequate sedation and analgesia, normothermia, and normocapnia (PaCO₂ 35–45). Next tiers include CSF drainage, hyperosmolar therapy (mannitol or hypertonic saline), and then advanced therapies. The goals are ICP below 22 mm Hg and CPP of 60–70 mm Hg (Brain Trauma Foundation).

Sources: Hickey & Strayer, Neuro Nursing, 8th ed.; BTF Severe TBI Guidelines, 4th ed. (2016)

Question 7

A 66-year-old with hypertension and type 2 diabetes had 25 minutes of weakness in her left arm and slurred speech that fully resolved before she arrived. Her neurologic exam is now normal, BP is 152/88 mm Hg, and a noncontrast CT shows no acute findings. She feels fine and asks to go home and see her own doctor next week. Which statement about her condition is MOST accurate?

  1. A CT with no infarct confirms that no brain injury has occurred
  2. Her ABCD2 score is low enough that clinic follow-up next week is reasonable
  3. Symptoms lasting over 10 minutes mean she has had a completed stroke
  4. Her stroke risk is highest over the next 24 to 48 hours

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. Tempting because the scan is clear, but noncontrast CT may not show an infarct for more than 24 hours; diffusion-weighted MRI is the most accurate test to rule one out.
  • B. Her score is 6 (age, blood pressure, weakness, duration and diabetes), which is high risk, so a week's delay leaves her unprotected during the period of greatest danger.
  • C. A TIA is defined by the absence of infarction on imaging, not by a time cutoff; most last under 5 minutes, but a 25-minute episode can still be a TIA.
  • D. Correct. Risk of stroke after a TIA is highest early, and with an ABCD2 score of 6 her 2-day risk is about 8%, so evaluation happens now, usually as an inpatient.

Merck defines a TIA as focal brain ischemia causing sudden, transient deficits without permanent infarction on imaging; symptoms usually last under an hour, and most under 5 minutes. Stroke risk is highest in the first 24 to 48 hours. ABCD2 gives 1 point for age 60 or older, 1 for SBP 140 or more or DBP above 90, 2 for weakness (1 for speech disturbance alone), 2 for 60 minutes or longer (1 for 10 to 59 minutes) and 1 for diabetes; her score of 6 carries about an 8% 2-day stroke risk. Merck advises rapid evaluation, usually inpatient with telemetry: diffusion-weighted MRI, vascular imaging of the carotid and cerebral arteries, and a search for atrial fibrillation, with antiplatelets and statins to prevent a stroke.

Sources: Merck Manual Professional: Transient Ischemic Attack (2026)

Question 8

A 36-year-old man had a diarrheal illness 2 weeks ago. Four days ago his feet began to tingle, and today he has trouble climbing stairs. He has symmetric weakness of both legs and absent ankle reflexes, and he can still walk unaided. A lumbar puncture in the ED shows CSF protein 38 mg/dL (reference 15 to 45 mg/dL) with 2 white cells/µL. The resident says the normal protein makes Guillain-Barré syndrome unlikely. Which interpretation of the CSF result is MOST accurate?

  1. A normal protein this early does not rule out Guillain-Barré syndrome
  2. The normal protein makes a functional cause of his weakness more likely
  3. The normal protein suggests his breathing muscles will stay strong
  4. IV immunoglobulin should wait until a repeat puncture shows a raised protein

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. Leonhard and colleagues report that CSF protein is still normal in 30 to 50% of patients in the first week and 10 to 30% in the second, so a normal value on day 4 does not exclude the diagnosis.
  • B. Tempting because a raised protein with a normal cell count is the classic finding, but ascending tingling, symmetric leg weakness and lost reflexes after a diarrheal illness fit Guillain-Barré syndrome, and the protein often rises later.
  • C. CSF protein does not measure respiratory risk. Leonhard says respiratory function should be monitored in every patient, since failure can occur without dyspnea and up to 22% need ventilation in the first week of admission.
  • D. The decision to treat rests on the clinical picture, not the CSF: Leonhard advises treatment once a patient cannot walk 10 m independently, and considering it sooner if weakness progresses rapidly or bulbar, autonomic or respiratory problems appear.

The classic CSF finding in Guillain-Barré syndrome is albumino-cytological dissociation: a raised protein with a normal cell count. Leonhard and colleagues stress that protein is normal in 30 to 50% of patients in the first week after onset and in 10 to 30% in the second week, so a normal value does not rule out the diagnosis; electrodiagnostic studies can also be normal within the first week. What the cell count does help with is the differential: marked pleocytosis above 50 cells/µL suggests another process, such as infection or malignancy of the nerve roots. Early in the illness the diagnosis is clinical, so the nurse watches for progression with serial strength checks and vital capacity, using the 20/30/40 rule (VC below 20 mL/kg) as a warning.

Sources: Leonhard et al., GBS Diagnosis and Management in Ten Steps (2019)

Question 9

A 74-year-old pedestrian struck by a car has a severe traumatic brain injury (GCS 7) and was intubated in the trauma bay. The FAST exam is negative and there is no external bleeding. Data are: BP 104/62 mm Hg, HR 88, SpO₂ 98%, T 36.6 °C. The resident says the blood pressure is acceptable because the systolic is above 90 mm Hg. Which response by the nurse is BEST?

  1. A systolic of 100 mm Hg is the brain injury target for his age group
  2. Keeping the systolic near 90 mm Hg limits bleeding until surgery
  3. His systolic pressure is below the 110 mm Hg target for his age
  4. A mean arterial pressure of 65 mm Hg is enough for him

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Tempting because 100 mm Hg is one of the guideline numbers, but it applies to patients 50 to 69 years old. Over 70, the suggested floor rises to 110 mm Hg.
  • B. Permissive hypotension is used for uncontrolled hemorrhage, not for brain injury. The injured brain depends on blood pressure for perfusion, so a low pressure adds secondary ischemic injury.
  • C. Correct. The Brain Trauma Foundation suggests keeping the systolic at 110 mm Hg or higher for patients 15 to 49 or over 70 years old. At 104 mm Hg this patient is hypotensive for a brain injury and needs treatment.
  • D. A MAP of 65 mm Hg is a sepsis target. It does not provide the cerebral perfusion pressure of about 60 mm Hg or more that the brain needs when ICP is raised.

In severe traumatic brain injury the brain often loses autoregulation, so cerebral blood flow follows systemic pressure, and hypotension adds ischemic secondary injury. The Brain Trauma Foundation (4th edition) suggests a systolic of at least 100 mm Hg for patients 50 to 69 years old and at least 110 mm Hg for those 15 to 49 or older than 70, which means the familiar trauma cutoff of 90 mm Hg is too low. Cerebral perfusion pressure equals MAP minus ICP; the guideline targets 60 to 70 mm Hg, and Merck notes that ischemia can follow when it falls below 50. With a negative FAST and no bleeding source, the nurse should report the pressure as below target and expect fluids or a vasopressor while the cause of the low pressure is sought.

Sources: BTF Severe TBI Guidelines, 4th ed. (2016); Merck Manual Professional: Traumatic Brain Injury (2026)

Question 10

A 71-year-old with long-standing ankylosing spondylitis fell backward from standing and struck his head on a door frame. He reports new neck pain and holds his head bent forward, as it has been for years. He moves and feels all four limbs normally. While a technician tries to lay him flat and fit a standard rigid collar, he cries out in pain. Which action should the nurse take?

  1. Support his head in its usual flexed position with pillows or towel rolls
  2. Stop spinal precautions, since a fall from standing is a low-energy mechanism
  3. Ease his neck back to neutral so that the standard collar fits
  4. Fit a larger rigid collar and keep him flat until the CT is done

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. Patients with severe kyphosis or ankylosing spondylitis may not tolerate a collar and are immobilized in a position of comfort with towel rolls or sandbags.
  • B. A ground-level fall can fracture a stiff, fused spine, and he has new neck pain, so he still needs spinal precautions and imaging; mechanism alone does not clear the spine.
  • C. Neutral alignment is the usual goal, which makes this tempting, but his spine has been fixed in flexion for years; forcing it toward neutral is painful and can move an injured, rigid segment.
  • D. A bigger collar still assumes a neck that can lie flat and straight. Laying him flat forces the fixed curve against the stretcher, which is what caused the pain.

NASEMSO's spinal care guideline notes that patients with severe kyphosis or ankylosing spondylitis may not tolerate a cervical collar and should be immobilized in a position of comfort using towel rolls or sandbags. Its general approach applies too: spinal precautions are a treatment for patients likely to benefit, an alert patient with pain will limit his own movement, and the patient should not be forced into a position for the sake of a device. His new midline pain after a fall keeps him in the group that needs precautions and imaging, because a rigid, fused spine can fracture with little force, so the nurse protects his usual posture rather than either straightening him or dropping precautions.

Sources: NASEMSO Model EMS Guidelines v3.0 (2022)
