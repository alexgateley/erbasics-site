Question 1

A 27-year-old patient reports a sexual assault by an unknown man 10 hours ago. She has never received hepatitis B vaccine, her pregnancy test is negative, and she will start HIV nonoccupational postexposure prophylaxis and presumptive STI treatment in the emergency department. The assailant's hepatitis B status cannot be determined. Which hepatitis B plan should the nurse anticipate?

  1. Hepatitis B vaccine today, with doses at 1 to 2 and 4 to 6 months
  2. Hepatitis B immune globulin today, with the vaccine series begun at follow-up
  3. Hepatitis B surface antigen testing now, vaccinating if it is negative at follow-up
  4. Hepatitis B vaccine and hepatitis B immune globulin today

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. When the assailant's status is unknown, CDC recommends postexposure vaccination without immune globulin, started at the initial exam and completed on schedule.
  • B. Tempting because immune globulin gives immediate passive protection, but it is not recommended when the assailant's status is unknown, and the vaccine is the part that should start today.
  • C. Tempting because baseline testing is part of the assault workup, but the first vaccine dose is given at the initial examination rather than deferred, so a missed follow-up does not leave her unprotected.
  • D. Tempting because the two are given together after some exposures, but CDC adds HBIG for an unvaccinated survivor only when the assailant is known to be HBsAg positive.

The CDC 2021 STI guidelines recommend hepatitis B vaccination for sexual assault survivors who are not already vaccinated, given at the initial examination, with follow-up doses at 1 to 2 and 4 to 6 months. HBIG is added only if the assailant is known to be HBsAg positive; Merck likewise states that HBIG is not given. The same visit covers emergency contraception, presumptive treatment for chlamydia, gonorrhea and trichomoniasis, HPV vaccine when age-eligible, and HIV nPEP when the exposure is within 72 hours, which is why the timing of each prophylaxis is checked separately.

Sources: CDC STI Treatment Guidelines (2021); Merck Manual Professional: Medical Examination of the Sexual Assault Victim (2026)

Question 2

A 67-year-old man with benign prostatic hyperplasia had his first episode of acute urinary retention, and catheterization drained 1,000 mL. He is afebrile, the urinalysis is clear and the creatinine is normal. Urology agrees that he can go home tonight. Which discharge plan should the nurse anticipate?

  1. Teach clean intermittent self-catheterization four times a day at home
  2. Catheter to a leg bag, tamsulosin and a voiding trial in 2 to 3 days
  3. Keep the catheter in place and book a prostate resection within 1 week
  4. Remove the catheter before he leaves and start daily finasteride

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Intermittent self-catheterization is used for chronic retention, such as a poorly contracting bladder, but after a first episode from BPH the usual plan is a short indwelling period with an alpha blocker and a voiding trial.
  • B. Correct. Starting an alpha blocker such as tamsulosin or alfuzosin at catheter insertion improves the chance of voiding successfully when the catheter comes out 2 to 3 days later.
  • C. Surgery may follow if retention recurs, but men with BPH-related retention should have at least one voiding trial after catheter removal before surgery is considered.
  • D. Finasteride can prevent recurrent retention over months in a man with an enlarged prostate, but it does nothing in the next few days, and removing the catheter tonight makes a failed void and repeat retention likely.

After acute retention from benign prostatic hyperplasia, the bladder is drained completely and the cause is addressed. Selius and Subedi report that an alpha blocker started at catheterization and given for about three days improves the success of a trial without catheter at 2 to 3 days, and that every man with BPH-related retention should have at least one voiding trial before surgery is considered. Five-alpha reductase inhibitors such as finasteride may prevent recurrence over the long term when the prostate is enlarged. The nurse teaches leg bag care, orthostatic precautions with the alpha blocker, and return for fever, a blocked catheter or no drainage.

Sources: AFP: Urinary Retention in Adults (2008); Merck Manual, Professional Version

Question 3

A 69-year-old patient with stage 4 chronic kidney disease has had left renal colic for 3 hours from a 4 mm distal ureteral stone and has vomited twice. Data are: creatinine 3.2 mg/dL (baseline 3.0), eGFR 19 mL/min/1.73 m2, HR 96/min, BP 150/88 mm Hg, pain 9/10. There is no history of peptic ulcer disease. Which analgesic order should the nurse anticipate?

  1. Hydromorphone IV in small, titrated doses
  2. Meperidine IM, repeated at 3- to 4-hour intervals as needed
  3. Ibuprofen 800 mg by mouth with food
  4. Ketorolac 15 mg IV, the reduced dose for renal impairment

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. When an NSAID cannot be used, EAU lists hydromorphone among the second-line options for acute colic; small titrated doses control the pain without the renal risk of an NSAID.
  • B. Meperidine (pethidine) was once a standard colic drug, but EAU advises avoiding it because it causes more vomiting, and this patient is already vomiting.
  • C. Taking an NSAID with food protects the stomach, but it does not remove the risk to a kidney with an eGFR of 19, and a patient who is vomiting is unlikely to keep an oral dose down.
  • D. NSAIDs are the first-line drugs for renal colic, and 15 mg IV is the label's single dose for patients with renal impairment, which makes this tempting. The same label contraindicates ketorolac in advanced renal impairment, which an eGFR of 19 represents.

The European Association of Urology makes an NSAID the first drug for renal colic (strong recommendation) but notes that NSAIDs can worsen renal function when the GFR is already reduced. Its second-line options for acute pain are hydromorphone, pentazocine, tramadol or ketamine, and it advises avoiding pethidine. The ketorolac label is explicit: it is contraindicated in advanced renal impairment and in patients at risk of renal failure from volume depletion, and even in milder impairment or age over 65 the dose is limited to 15 mg IV and 60 mg a day for no more than 5 days. Ondansetron treats the vomiting, and a 4 mm stone is likely to pass.

Sources: EAU Guidelines on Urolithiasis; FDA Prescribing Information (DailyMed); Merck Manual Professional: Urinary Calculi (2025)

Question 4

Four patients arrive at triage with sudden one-sided lower abdominal pain and nausea. Which patient's history places her at GREATEST risk for adnexal torsion?

  1. Ovarian stimulation with gonadotropins for in vitro fertilization 5 days ago
  2. Chlamydial cervicitis treated 2 months ago, with a new partner since
  3. A copper intrauterine device placed 3 years ago, with regular menses
  4. A 2-cm simple ovarian cyst seen on a scan 3 years after menopause

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. Ovulation induction enlarges the ovaries with multiple follicles, and an ovary larger than about 4 cm is the main risk factor for twisting on its supporting ligaments.
  • B. This history raises the risk of pelvic inflammatory disease, which typically causes bilateral pelvic pain, fever and cervical motion tenderness rather than torsion.
  • C. A long-standing copper IUD with normal cycles does not enlarge the ovary or make it more mobile, so it does not raise the risk of torsion.
  • D. A known cyst draws attention to the ovary, but a 2-cm cyst does not enlarge it enough to raise torsion risk. The concern is enlargement beyond about 4 cm, usually from a benign mass.

Adnexal torsion is twisting of the ovary, often with the fallopian tube, which cuts off its blood supply and can destroy ovarian function if not relieved. The strongest risk factor is ovarian enlargement beyond about 4 cm, most often from a benign cyst or tumor. Other risks include ovulation induction, pregnancy with a large corpus luteum cyst and a previous torsion. Torsion of a normal-sized ovary is rare in adults but more common in children and adolescents, so a normal ovary does not exclude it in a young patient.

Sources: Merck Manual, Professional Version; Sheehy's Manual of Emergency Care, 8th ed.; Maternity & Women's Health Care, 13th ed.

Question 5

A 14-year-old boy has had right scrotal pain for 5 hours and has vomited once. On examination the right testis is swollen but not hard, it is not high-riding, and the cremasteric reflex is present. Based on his TWIST (Testicular Workup for Ischemia and Suspected Torsion) score, which action is MOST appropriate next?

  1. Obtain an emergent color Doppler ultrasound of the scrotum
  2. Take him straight to the operating room for exploration without imaging
  3. Discharge with ibuprofen, scrotal support and urology follow-up in 2 days
  4. Send a urinalysis and start empiric antibiotics for epididymitis

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. Swelling (2 points) plus vomiting (1 point) gives 3, the intermediate band, in which Doppler ultrasound decides whether torsion is present.
  • B. Going directly to surgery fits a high-risk score of 5 to 7 or an unmistakable presentation; at 3 points imaging should come first, done quickly.
  • C. TWIST suggests ultrasound may be avoidable only at a low-risk score of 0 to 2; at 3 points torsion has not been excluded, so discharge without imaging is unsafe.
  • D. Epididymitis is possible in an adolescent, but torsion must be excluded first; starting antibiotics without imaging risks losing the testis during the ischemic window.

The TWIST score adds testicular swelling (2), a hard testis (2), an absent cremasteric reflex (1), nausea or vomiting (1) and a high-riding testis (1). Scores of 0 to 2 are low risk, 3 to 4 intermediate and 5 to 7 high risk; ultrasound is mainly needed in the intermediate band, while a high score supports going straight to surgical exploration. The score supports judgment rather than replacing Doppler, so urology is notified early because testicular salvage falls sharply after about 6 hours of ischemia.

Sources: Sheehy's Manual of Emergency Care, 8th ed.; Wong's Care of Infants & Children, 12th ed.

Question 6

A 10-year-old boy with sickle cell disease (HbSS) has had a painful erection for 5 hours despite oral fluids and ibuprofen at home. Data are: HR 116, BP 110/66, SpO2 97% on room air, Hb 7.8 g/dL (baseline 8.0). IV morphine has been ordered. Which intervention should the nurse anticipate NEXT?

  1. Oral pseudoephedrine and a warm bath, then reassessment in 1 hour
  2. Urgent urology evaluation for aspiration and phenylephrine
  3. Exchange transfusion to lower the hemoglobin S percentage
  4. IV fluids at 1.5 times maintenance and reassessment in 2 hours

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Oral agents and home measures are unlikely to reverse ischemic priapism that has already lasted 5 hours, and waiting delays definitive treatment.
  • B. Correct. An erection lasting more than 4 hours in sickle cell disease is ischemic priapism, and standard treatment, corporal aspiration with intracavernosal phenylephrine, should not wait for disease-specific therapy.
  • C. Exchange transfusion treats some sickle cell complications, but in priapism it is second line, has been linked to neurological events, and aspiration should not wait for it.
  • D. Hydration and analgesia support the child, but systemic sickle cell measures should not delay standard treatment, and 2 more hours of ischemia raises the risk of erectile dysfunction.

Hematologic disorders, chiefly sickle cell disease, are among the most common causes of priapism in children, and priapism is most common in boys aged 5 to 10 years. Sickled cells obstruct venous outflow from the corpora, the trapped blood becomes ischemic, and prolonged or repeated episodes can cause fibrosis and permanent erectile dysfunction. The Scottish paediatric sickle cell guideline (SPAH 2024) makes aspiration and irrigation, then intracavernosal phenylephrine, the treatment for an episode lasting more than 4 hours, with hydration, analgesia and oxygen alongside and urology involved without delay; exchange transfusion is second line, has been linked to neurological events, and surgical treatment should not be delayed for transfusion. Urology sets the phenylephrine dose for a child, and the nurse monitors blood pressure and heart rate during injections.

Sources: SPAH Network: Priapism in Sickle Cell Disease, Paediatric Guideline (2024); Merck Manual, Professional Version; Wong's Care of Infants & Children, 12th ed.; ENA ENPC Provider Manual, 6th ed.

Question 7

A 38-year-old patient has soaked a large pad every 20 minutes for 3 hours and feels faint when standing. She takes no medicines and has no history of blood clots. Data are: HR 124/min, BP 92/58 mm Hg, RR 22/min, hemoglobin 8.9 g/dL, urine hCG negative. Which plan should the nurse anticipate?

  1. Transvaginal ultrasound before any treatment to find a structural cause
  2. Oral medroxyprogesterone, iron and gynecology follow-up within the week
  3. IV fluids, blood products as needed and IV conjugated estrogen
  4. Methylergonovine IM, with fundal massage if the bleeding continues

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Tempting because fibroids and polyps are common causes, but imaging can follow stabilization; delaying treatment in an unstable patient is the error.
  • B. Tempting because oral hormones are a standard regimen for AUB, but this patient is tachycardic and hypotensive, so outpatient therapy is unsafe.
  • C. Correct. She is in early hemorrhagic shock, so IV fluid and blood products come first, with IV conjugated estrogen or tranexamic acid to slow the bleeding and an urgent gynecology consult.
  • D. Tempting because it is a familiar uterotonic for heavy bleeding, but it is a postpartum atony drug, and fundal massage has no role in a nonpregnant uterus.

Acute AUB with tachycardia, hypotension and orthostatic symptoms is a hemorrhage emergency. The Merck Manual manages it like hemorrhage from any site: close monitoring of vital signs and hemodynamic stabilization with IV crystalloid and blood products as needed, with a CBC and labs for disseminated intravascular coagulation. Two large-bore IVs and a type and crossmatch are standard hemorrhage practice rather than Merck wording. Merck lists conjugated estrogens 25 mg IV every 4 to 6 hours (up to 4 doses) or tranexamic acid as acute medical options, with intrauterine tamponade, D&C or uterine artery embolization when bleeding persists. High-dose estrogen and tranexamic acid raise the risk of thrombosis, which is why the absence of clot history in the stem matters. A negative hCG excludes pregnancy-related bleeding.

Sources: Merck Manual Professional: Abnormal Uterine Bleeding (2026)

Question 8

Forty minutes after a precipitous birth in the emergency department, the nurse weighs the pads, underpads and drapes and calculates a cumulative quantified blood loss of 1,050 mL, with amniotic fluid already excluded. The fundus is now firm and the bleeding has slowed to a trickle. Data are: HR 92/min, BP 116/74 mm Hg, SpO2 99% on room air. Which action is MOST appropriate?

  1. Repeat the count by visual estimate, since weighing tends to overstate loss
  2. Recheck vital signs in 15 minutes and call the team if the heart rate rises
  3. Record a normal loss for a vaginal birth and resume routine checks
  4. Activate the hemorrhage protocol, since the loss meets the definition

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. Tempting as a double check, but visual estimation is the method that misses blood loss; weighing and graduated drapes are more accurate, which is why quantification is recommended.
  • B. Tempting because she looks stable, but pregnancy's expanded blood volume lets a patient lose more than 1,000 mL before tachycardia or hypotension appear, so waiting for vital signs to change delays care.
  • C. Tempting because older teaching tied the threshold to the mode of birth, but the current definition no longer separates vaginal from cesarean birth, and 1,050 mL crosses it.
  • D. Correct. A cumulative loss of more than 1,000 mL within 24 hours of birth is postpartum hemorrhage whether or not signs of hypovolemia are present, so the response team, labs and blood readiness start now.

Merck defines postpartum hemorrhage as blood loss of more than 1,000 mL, or blood loss with signs or symptoms of hypovolemia, within 24 hours of birth, and StatPearls notes that the 2017 ACOG revision applies regardless of the route of delivery. StatPearls notes that pregnant patients can lose more than 1,000 mL before vital signs change, and that signs of hypovolemia usually mean about 1,500 mL is gone. Because quantified loss is the trigger, the nurse activates the hemorrhage response, sends a CBC, fibrinogen and type and crossmatch, keeps quantifying and checks vital signs every 15 minutes.

Sources: StatPearls (NCBI Bookshelf); Merck Manual Professional: Postpartum Hemorrhage (2026); StatPearls: Postpartum Hemorrhage (2024)

Question 9

A 27-year-old man comes to triage 2 hours after he heard a cracking sound and felt sudden pain when his erect penis bent during intercourse. The shaft is swollen, bruised and deviated to one side. He has voided clear urine without difficulty, and there is no blood at the meatus. He asks for ice so he can go home. Which plan should the nurse anticipate?

  1. Urologic consultation for surgical exploration and repair
  2. Ice, elevation and a urology clinic visit within the week
  3. An indwelling urethral catheter to rest the urethra
  4. Retrograde urethrography before any other evaluation

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. Merck advises surgical exploration and repair for all penile fractures; the diagnosis is usually clinical, as it is here.
  • B. Swelling and bruising can look like a bad contusion that will settle at home, but a cracking sound with deformity means a ruptured corpus cavernosum that needs surgical repair.
  • C. A catheter does not treat a ruptured corpus cavernosum, and a man who voids clear urine easily has no urethral reason for one; placed into an unrecognized urethral tear, a catheter can turn a partial tear into a complete one.
  • D. A urethrogram is added when gross hematuria, blood at the meatus or inability to void suggests a urethral tear. He voids clear urine without difficulty, so it is not the first step here.

Penile fracture is a rupture of the corpus cavernosum, most often when the erect penis is forcibly bent during sex. The history of a cracking sound, immediate pain, marked swelling, bruising and deformity makes the diagnosis clinical; ultrasound or MRI is used only when it is unclear. Merck advises surgical exploration and repair of every penile fracture, and its urethral trauma page lists penile fracture among the injuries that get immediate open repair. Hematuria, blood at the meatus or inability to void suggests an associated urethral tear, which calls for retrograde urethrography before any catheter.

Sources: Merck Manual, Professional Version

Question 10

A 29-year-old at 22 weeks' gestation has had 4 hours of colicky right flank pain radiating to the groin, and the urine dipstick shows blood. Data are: T 37.1 °C (98.8 °F), HR 102/min, BP 118/70 mm Hg, fetal heart rate 145/min. A student nurse says that a noncontrast CT is the standard test for kidney stones. Which imaging study should the nurse anticipate FIRST?

  1. MRI urography without gadolinium contrast
  2. Low-dose noncontrast CT of the abdomen and pelvis
  3. Plain abdominal radiograph of kidneys, ureters and bladder
  4. Ultrasound of the kidneys and bladder

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. Tempting because MRI avoids radiation, but it is the second-line study, used when ultrasound is not diagnostic.
  • B. Tempting because noncontrast CT is the reference test for stones outside pregnancy, but in pregnancy it is reserved as the last-line option after ultrasound and MRI.
  • C. Tempting because it is quick and low dose, but it misses many stones, exposes the fetus to radiation and is not recommended as the first study in pregnancy.
  • D. Correct. Ultrasound avoids ionizing radiation and is the preferred first imaging method for suspected stones in pregnancy.

The EAU urolithiasis guideline gives a strong recommendation to use ultrasound as the preferred imaging method in pregnant women, MRI as the second-line modality, and low-dose CT only as a last-line option. Ultrasound may show hydronephrosis, which needs careful reading because mild right-sided dilation is common in normal pregnancy. While imaging is arranged, the emergency nurse keeps the pregnancy in view: obtain a urine culture, report fever promptly because an infected obstructed kidney is an emergency, and watch for contractions or fetal heart rate changes, with obstetrics involved early.

Sources: EAU Guidelines on Urolithiasis
