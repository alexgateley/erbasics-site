Nurse–client relationship

PhaseNurse’s work
Pre-orientationReview the client’s data; examine your own feelings, biases and anxieties
OrientationIntroduce self; learn needs and values; build trust and rapport; discuss the expected end
WorkingMost of the time: listen, assess, set goals together, teach
TerminationShift end or discharge: review progress, goals, referrals; prepare the client for the end

Boundaries: the focus stays on the client; no social, romantic or financial ties. Answer a personal question briefly, then return to the client.

Crisis and de-escalation

Crisis: usual coping fails; time-limited, days up to 4–6 weeks. Goals: safety, support, return to the prior level of function.

  1. Assess safety: suicide, violence, medical risk
  2. Make contact; build rapport
  3. Define the problem and its trigger
  4. Explore feelings; listen actively
  5. Explore past coping and supports
  6. Act on a plan with supports and referrals
  7. Follow up
  • Escalating: calm voice, short sentences, one speaker, offer choices
  • Stay ≥2 arm’s lengths away; keep your exit clear
  • Set limits matter-of-factly; never threaten
  • Restraint only when nothing less restrictive keeps people safe (see Mental Health: Meds and Communication)

Suicide screening and safety

  • Ask directly; asking does not plant the idea
  • ASQ: 4 yes/no questions, about 20 seconds. Any yes, then ask “thoughts of killing yourself right now?”
  • C-SSRS: intent or plan in the past month, or any attempt or preparation in 3 months: high risk
  • Safety plan in the client’s words; no-suicide contracts lack evidence
  • Discharge is high risk: limit lethal means, early follow-up, 988 (call or text)
Safety Yes to “right now,” or a plan with access to means: keep the client in sight, remove dangerous objects, alert the provider. No leaving before a STAT safety evaluation.

Grief and culture

  • Kübler-Ross: denial, anger, bargaining, depression, acceptance; any order, may recur
  • Normal grief: yearning, crying, sensing the person; support it, do not medicate it
  • Anticipatory grief starts before a loss; disenfranchised grief goes unrecognized
  • Prolonged grief disorder: disabling grief ≥12 months after a death (≥6 months in children)
  • Culture: ask, do not assume; rituals, body care, eye contact and decision-makers vary
  • Use a trained interpreter; avoid children and untrained relatives

Answering “best response” items

  1. Safety first: a suicide or violence cue gets a direct question or action
  2. Choose the option that names the feeling or invites more talk
  3. Cross out blocks: why, reassurance, advice, defending, changing the subject
  4. Psychosis: do not argue or agree; present reality, answer the feeling
  5. Severe anxiety or panic: stay, short simple directions, fewer stimuli
  6. Dangerous misunderstanding: clarify or teach now
Exam tip Stems ask for the “most therapeutic,” “best” or “most appropriate” response. Accept early denial and return later, but teach survival skills now.

Why therapeutic communication works

Therapeutic communication keeps the conversation on the client’s needs rather than the nurse’s comfort. The 2026 NCLEX-RN test plan places it in Psychosocial Integrity (6–12% of items) and lists the activities it tests: assess verbal and nonverbal communication needs, respect the client’s values, allow time, encourage the client to verbalize feelings such as fear, and evaluate whether the communication worked.

Active listening shows interest in both words and body language and checks understanding by restating. Open RN’s SOLER cue covers the posture: sit squarely facing the client, keep an open posture, lean in, hold good eye contact, and stay relaxed. Nonverbal cues are less consciously controlled than words, so they are often the more accurate data: a client who says they are not in pain but winces is assessed further. When words and behavior disagree, the nurse names the observation (“You say you’re fine, but your hands are shaking”).

Eye contact, nodding, touch and silence mean different things across cultures. In some cultures direct eye contact with an authority figure is disrespectful, and nodding can mean “thank you” rather than “yes.” The nurse asks how the client prefers to be approached and touched, and checks understanding with teach-back rather than reading agreement into a nod.

Confidentiality is explained early in the relationship: what the client shares stays within the treatment team, but a plan to harm self or others is shared for safety. An option in which the nurse promises to keep a secret from the team is a trap answer.

Techniques and blocks in action

Worked example: a client scheduled for surgery says, “I’m so nervous I can’t think straight.” Which response is best? “Tell me what worries you most about tomorrow” is open-ended and lets the nurse find the specific fear, which can then be taught to or passed to the surgeon. “Your surgeon is excellent” is false reassurance, “Why are you nervous?” asks for a justification, and “Try some deep breathing” gives advice before assessing.

Worked example: a client just told of a cancer diagnosis says angrily, “The lab mixed up my results.” The best response accepts the reaction and stays available: “This is a lot to take in. I’m here when you want to talk.” Reciting the biopsy findings argues with denial and raises defensiveness; telling the client to calm down judges the anger.

Worked example: a client with schizophrenia says the television is sending messages that staff are poisoning the food. The nurse neither argues (“That’s not true”) nor plays along. Presenting reality and answering the feeling is therapeutic: “I don’t see messages on the TV, and I know it must feel frightening.” Open RN also lists encouraging the client to describe the perception (“What are you hearing now?”).

Open RN adds other techniques that appear in answer options: acceptance (“Yes, I understand”), giving recognition without praise (“I noticed you took all your medications”), making observations (“You look tired today”), exploring, encouraging comparisons with past coping, and offering hope. Confronting an inconsistency is therapeutic only in the working phase, after trust exists. Its nontherapeutic list adds personal questions asked out of curiosity, stereotypes (“Older adults are always confused”), minimizing or false reassurance (“At least you still have your other children”), sympathy that centers the nurse’s feelings, passive or aggressive responses, arguing, and so many probing questions that the client feels interrogated.

Exam tip When two options both sound kind, pick the one that keeps the client talking about their own feelings. An option that makes the nurse, the staff or the provider the subject is wrong.

Defense mechanisms: telling look-alikes apart

Defense mechanisms protect a person from the anxiety that stress and conflict create, but excessive use is linked with specific mental health disorders (Open RN). StatPearls groups them as primitive (denial, projection, regression, repression, splitting, conversion, acting out) and higher-level (sublimation, suppression, compensation, intellectualization, rationalization, reaction formation, displacement, humor). The test plan asks nurses to recognize them and to evaluate whether a client uses them constructively.

Look-alikesKey difference
Repression vs suppressionRepression is unconscious blocking; suppression is a conscious choice to set aside
Projection vs displacementProjection puts one’s own feeling on another person; displacement moves a feeling to a safer target
Rationalization vs intellectualizationRationalization excuses a behavior; intellectualization replaces feelings with facts
Reaction formation vs undoingReaction formation acts the opposite of the feeling; undoing tries to cancel an act already done
Sublimation vs compensationSublimation redirects a drive into an accepted outlet; compensation excels in one area to offset another
Identification vs regressionIdentification copies an admired person; regression returns to earlier behavior

Splitting, seeing people as all good or all bad, is common in borderline personality disorder (“You’re the only nurse who understands me”). The answer is consistency: one plan of care and the same limits from every staff member. Conversion presents as a physical symptom with no physical explanation (functional neurologic symptom disorder); the nurse still assesses for a medical cause and treats the symptom as real to the client.

When to step in: early denial after bad news is protective and usually fades, so the nurse accepts it and returns to the topic. Denial that blocks safety is different. A client with new type 1 diabetes who says the doctors probably made a mistake still gets the survival skills now (insulin, recognizing low glucose), simply and with family present, and readiness to learn is reassessed at each contact.

The relationship, boundaries and transference

Peplau described three overlapping phases: orientation, working and termination. Many nursing texts add a pre-orientation (pre-interaction) step in which the nurse studies the client’s data before the first visit (Moykari 2023) and also examines their own feelings, biases and anxieties so they do not color the first meeting. In orientation the nurse introduces themself, learns the client’s needs and values, protects privacy, builds trust and rapport, and discusses when and how the relationship is expected to end so that termination is not a surprise. Most of the nurse’s time is spent in the working phase: active listening, assessment, goals and a plan set with the client, and teaching. Termination usually comes at the end of a shift or at discharge; the nurse reviews progress and post-discharge goals, makes referrals, prepares the client for the end, and should expect that the client may try to slip back into the working phase to avoid ending (Open RN). Relationships range from a few minutes in an emergency to several months in residential care.

Boundaries protect the client. Therapeutic communication has a purpose, unlike casual social talk (Open RN). NCSBN describes the therapeutic relationship as designed to meet the patient’s needs; boundary violations arise when the nurse’s needs and the patient’s get confused, and show up as excessive personal disclosure, secrecy or a reversal of roles. Sexual misconduct is the extreme form. The nurse avoids personal, social or business relationships with clients and does not share personal problems. If asked a personal question, the nurse answers briefly and turns back: “Yes, I have two children. Tell me about yours.” Boundary violations can be grounds for board discipline. A nurse who finds herself visiting a client on a day off has crossed a boundary and should reflect, consult a supervisor or trusted colleague, and document.

Transference is the client seeing the nurse through feelings carried over from an earlier important relationship; countertransference is the thoughts and feelings the client stirs up in the nurse. Both are present in every therapeutic relationship (Prasko 2022). The nurse notices them, avoids taking the client’s remarks personally, and uses supervision and peer feedback so the relationship stays therapeutic.

Anxiety levels and crisis intervention

Learning and problem-solving are not possible at severe or panic levels; teach once anxiety falls.
Level (Peplau)What the nurse seesNursing approach
MildSharp focus; restless, fidgetyCalm presence, acknowledge feelings, listen
ModerateNarrowed field; learning hampered but possibleQuiet room, open-ended questions, past coping, activity
SevereFixed on one detail or scattered; cannot learn or problem-solveStay; firm, short, simple statements; nurse directs
PanicCannot process; may lose touch with realitySafety first, stay, meet physical needs, medication as ordered

A crisis is an inability to cope with or adapt to a stressor. Open RN groups crises as maturational (expected life transitions such as a new baby), situational (job loss, new diagnosis, divorce) and social or adventitious (disasters, violence). A crisis is time-limited, usually days and no longer than 4–6 weeks. The goals are safety, support with a return to the prior level of functioning as quickly as possible, and less harm to future mental health. A realistic view of the event, adequate support and adequate coping help a client resolve it.

Roberts’ seven-stage model (summarized by Tripathi 2023) orders the work: assess, including suicide and homicide risk; make contact and build rapport; define the problem and its precipitant; explore feelings with active listening; explore past coping and alternatives; carry out an action plan with supports and referrals; and follow up. Crisis work is brief and focused on the present problem, not on long-standing personality issues.

Worked example: a client whose house burned down last night cannot concentrate and says, “I don’t know what to do first.” The best action helps the client name the most urgent needs, breaks them into small steps and calls in support. Exploring childhood losses or teaching a long-term stress program does not fit an acute crisis.

De-escalation and violence risk

Most violence is preceded by warning signs, and more cues mean higher risk (Open RN 2e): loud speech, swearing or a threatening tone; pacing, clenched fists, arms held tightly across the chest, heavy breathing, a fixed stare, throwing objects, confusion or signs of intoxication. Mealtimes, shift changes and transports are higher-risk times. The nurse notes exits, stays positioned to leave quickly, and avoids wearing a stethoscope or necklace around the neck.

Project BETA (Richmond 2012) lists ten domains of verbal de-escalation: respect personal space (at least two arm’s lengths), do not provoke, have one person establish verbal contact, be concise and repeat, identify wants and feelings, listen closely, agree or agree to disagree, set clear limits with respectful consequences, offer choices and optimism, and debrief the patient and staff after any coercive measure. The aim is to help the patient calm himself and avoid restraint.

Worked example: a client on a psychiatric unit is pacing, clenching his fists and shouting at another client. First, move the other clients away, approach calmly at a distance and offer a quieter space. Touching the client, crowding him with staff or announcing restraint escalates the situation. Seclusion or restraint is a last resort, used only when no other option keeps the client or others safe (Open RN), and follows the order and monitoring rules on the Mental Health: Meds and Communication page.

Suicide screening, assessment and safety planning

Screening uses a validated tool. The NIMH Ask Suicide-Screening Questions (ASQ) takes about 20 seconds and is meant for medical patients aged 8 and up. It asks about wishing to be dead, feeling that family would be better off, thoughts of killing oneself in the past week, and past attempts. Any yes, or a refusal to answer, leads to the acuity question: “Are you having thoughts of killing yourself right now?” Yes to that is an acute positive: STAT safety and full mental health evaluation, the patient cannot leave, stays in sight, and dangerous objects are removed. A non-acute positive still needs a brief suicide safety assessment before the patient leaves. Youth are screened without a parent present when possible.

The Columbia Protocol (C-SSRS) uses plain-language questions that anyone can ask without mental health training. The most worrisome answers are a past-month yes to the intent or plan questions, or any suicidal behavior in the past 3 months; Columbia says the next steps depend on each setting’s resources and policies. Risk rises when a plan is specific and the method is lethal and accessible (Open RN 2e).

Worked example: a client with depression says, “Everyone would be better off without me.” The best response is a direct question: “Are you thinking about killing yourself?” Reassurance (“Your family loves you”), changing the subject, or asking why all miss the cue. Asking does not put the idea in the client’s head.

A safety plan is a short, prioritized written list of warning signs, coping strategies and people and services to contact, written in the client’s own words; the nurse problem-solves barriers to using it and agrees where it will be kept (Open RN 2e). In VA emergency departments, a safety plan plus at least two follow-up calls was associated with less suicidal behavior over 6 months, 3.03% vs 5.29% (Stanley 2018). No-suicide contracts have no evidence of effectiveness (Rudd 2006) and are never the answer.

Warning signs that should prompt reassessment include talk of being a burden, withdrawing, saying goodbye and giving away important items (NIMH). Antidepressant labels call for close monitoring for worsening and new suicidal thoughts, especially in the first few months and after dose changes (FDA boxed warning); the usual teaching explanation is that energy returns before hopelessness lifts. Risk stays high right after discharge, so the plan includes lethal-means counseling, early follow-up and the 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline.

Grief, loss and culture

Kübler-Ross described denial, anger, bargaining, depression and acceptance from interviews with dying patients (1969). She did not intend a fixed sequence; people show several at once, move back and forth or skip some, and pushing a client “toward acceptance” can harm (StatPearls). Worden’s model describes tasks instead: accept the reality of the loss, experience the pain, adjust to the environment, and redirect emotional energy.

Grief is the emotional response to loss; mourning is its outward, culturally shaped expression; bereavement is the grief and mourning that follow a death (Open RN Fundamentals). Anticipatory grief begins before an expected loss. Disenfranchised grief follows a loss others do not recognize, such as a stillbirth, a terminated pregnancy or a death from a stigmatized illness. Normal grief includes yearning, crying, dreams of the person and sensing their presence. It is supported; most bereaved people need no mental health treatment, and no medication treats grief itself (APA 2025).

Prolonged grief disorder (DSM-5-TR, per APA 2025) requires a death at least 12 months earlier in adults (6 months in children and adolescents), intense longing or preoccupation, and at least 3 of 8 symptoms nearly every day for the past month, such as identity disruption, marked disbelief, emotional numbness or intense loneliness, beyond cultural norms. APA estimates 4–15% of bereaved adults are affected.

Worked example: a client with terminal cancer says, “If I could just live until my daughter’s wedding, I’d accept anything after that.” This is bargaining. The nurse listens without judgment and supports realistic goals, such as helping plan for the client to attend.

Culture shapes mourning, body care and who makes decisions. Open RN’s examples include burial as soon as possible in Jewish and Muslim practice, same-gender preparation of the body in Muslim practice, and not leaving the body alone before cremation in Hindu practice, but practice varies within every group, so the nurse asks the client and family. Some clients prefer that family make decisions; the nurse asks the client privately how they want information shared. The National CLAS Standards say untrained individuals and minors should not be used as interpreters.

Exam pearls

  • Hopelessness or “better off without me”: ask directly about suicide before anything else.
  • The first months of an antidepressant and any dose change need close watching for suicidal thoughts, especially as energy returns.
  • “Why” questions, reassurance, advice and changing the subject are almost never the answer.
  • Hallucinations and delusions: do not argue, do not agree; state reality and respond to the feeling.
  • Severe anxiety or panic: short, simple directions and a quiet space; teaching waits.
  • Only suppression is conscious. Repression is unconscious forgetting.
  • Projection blames others for one’s own feeling; displacement aims the feeling at a safer target.
  • Termination is planned from the start (the expected end is discussed in orientation) and comes at shift end or discharge: prepare the client, review progress and refer; a personal relationship or keeping in touch afterward crosses a boundary.
  • Crisis care is brief and present-focused: safety, the immediate problem, supports, follow-up.
  • Safety plan, not a no-suicide contract; 988 by call or text.
  • Grief stages occur in any order; normal grief is supported, not medicated; grief disabling at 12 months needs evaluation.
  • Use a trained interpreter, not the client’s child.
