Swallowing and NPO

  • Stroke or new swallow problem: NPO, oral meds included, until a swallow screen is passed
  • Meals: fully upright, chin tuck, small bites on the stronger side, check for pocketing, stay upright after
  • Only the ordered thickness; straws only if speech therapy allows
  • Elective fasting (ASA): clear liquids 2 h, breast milk 4 h, formula or light meal 6 h, fatty food or meat 8 h
  • NPO until the gag reflex returns after endoscopy or bronchoscopy
Safety Coughing, a wet voice or pocketing at a meal: stop feeding, sit the client up, suction if needed, report. Aspiration can be silent.

Diets as orders, and the liquid progression

A therapeutic diet is a prescription. The nurse checks the tray against the order, allergies and swallow plan, helps the client eat, records intake and teaches. The 2026 NCLEX-RN test plan lists evaluating nutritional status and intake and output under Basic Care and Comfort. Items rarely ask for a nutrient number; they ask which food breaks the diet, which statement shows understanding, or what to do first.

Clear liquids leave little residue and are used before tests and some surgeries, briefly after stomach or bowel surgery, and for vomiting or acute pancreatitis (MedlinePlus). They lack calories and protein, so healthy people should not stay on them more than 3 to 4 days. Pulp, nectar, milk and yogurt are not clear, and red liquids may be stopped before a colonoscopy. Full liquids add cream, custard, pudding, ice cream, strained cream soups, juices with pulp and supplements: about 1,350 to 1,500 kcal and 45 g of protein a day, but little fiber.

The old ladder (clear, full, soft, regular) is not always needed. For mild acute pancreatitis, ACG 2024 suggests oral feeding within 24 to 48 hours rather than NPO, starting with a low-fat solid diet. Advance as ordered and as tolerated: no nausea, vomiting or distention.

Worked example. A client on clear liquids before a colonoscopy asks for something to eat. Which item can the nurse offer: orange juice with pulp, lemon gelatin, cherry popsicle or vanilla pudding? Lemon gelatin. Pulp and pudding are not clear liquids, and red-colored liquids may be avoided before the procedure.

Dysphagia: IDDSI levels and safe meals

The International Dysphagia Diet Standardisation Initiative (IDDSI) replaced dozens of local names with eight numbered, color-coded levels (0 to 7). Drinks run from 0 to 4 and foods from 3 to 7, so levels 3 and 4 describe both. Each level has a simple test, and the test, not the description, decides the level (IDDSI 2.2, April 2026).

Child sizes: level 5 lumps ≤2 mm wide, level 6 pieces ≤8 mm. The older US drink names (thin, nectar, honey, pudding) map only roughly to levels 0, 2, 3 and 4.

3Moderately thick / liquidisedOver 8 mL left; drips slowly through a fork
4Extremely thick / pureedHolds shape on a spoon, falls off when tilted; no lumps, not sticky

6Soft & bite-sizedPieces ≤1.5 cm; fork pressure that blanches the thumbnail squashes it

Thickened drinks and texture-modified foods lower the risk of aspiration but do not remove it (ASHA). Coughing or a wet, gurgly voice during or after eating are warning signs, but silent aspiration gives none, so a client who failed a screen stays NPO until speech therapy sets the diet. Chin-down posture helps some clients.

Nursing measures from fundamentals texts: fully upright, small bites and sips of the ordered consistency, food on the stronger side after a stroke, check for pocketing, no straws unless allowed, oral care, and stay upright after the meal. Ice cream and gelatin melt into thin liquid, so they are off thickened-liquid diets unless speech therapy allows them.

Worked example. A client after a stroke is on minced and moist food with mildly thick drinks. Halfway through lunch the voice sounds wet. First action? Stop the meal, keep the client upright, have the client cough, suction if needed, and report. Thickening drinks on your own or offering water to wash food down are wrong.

NPO and fasting before procedures

Sedation and anesthesia blunt airway reflexes, so a full stomach can be aspirated. ASA times for healthy clients having elective procedures (2017): clear liquids 2 h, breast milk 4 h, infant formula and non-human milk 6 h, a light meal 6 h, fried or fatty food or meat 8 h, for adults and children alike; the 2023 update keeps these but words the last as more than 6 h (De Meyer 2026). 'NPO after midnight' is older, stricter practice; follow the order.

Chewing gum does not cancel elective surgery but must come out before induction. GLP-1 receptor agonists (semaglutide, liraglutide, dulaglutide) slow gastric emptying; multisociety guidance including the ASA lets most clients continue them, with a 24-hour liquid diet for higher-risk clients. The nurse asks about these drugs and reports nausea, vomiting or bloating on the day of a procedure.

Other NPO situations: after a stroke until the swallow screen; after endoscopy or bronchoscopy until the gag reflex returns; suspected bowel obstruction or perforation. A client with diabetes who is NPO keeps basal insulin, mealtime insulin is held, correction insulin is given every 4 to 6 hours, and hypoglycemia is treated with IV glucose or glucagon (ADA 2026).

Worked example. A healthy adult is scheduled for elective surgery at 1000. Which report needs follow-up: black coffee at 0700, apple juice at 0745, toast at 0300, or bacon and eggs at 0500? Bacon and eggs at 0500: a fatty meal needs 8 hours. Black coffee and clear juice need 2 hours, and a light meal needs 6.

Sodium, DASH and fluid limits

The Daily Value for sodium is under 2,300 mg; Americans average about 3,400 mg, over 70% from packaged and prepared foods (FDA). On labels, 5% DV or less per serving is low and 20% or more is high. Sodium-free is under 5 mg, very low 35 mg or less, low 140 mg or less; 'reduced' means 25% less than the regular product, and 'no salt added' foods can still contain sodium.

DASH (NHLBI 2026) is an eating pattern: vegetables, fruit, whole grains, low-fat dairy, fish, poultry, beans and nuts, with less saturated fat, sweets and sugary drinks. It is rich in potassium, calcium, magnesium and fiber. Sodium 2,300 mg a day lowers blood pressure; 1,500 mg lowers it more.

Heart failure: the 2022 AHA/ACC/HFSA guideline calls avoiding excess sodium reasonable (2a) without a number. In SODIUM-HF (2022), a target under 1,500 mg a day did not reduce deaths, admissions or ED visits. Fluid restriction of 1.5 to 2 L a day relieves symptoms in severe heart failure (HFA 2021); AHA/ACC calls its benefit uncertain (2b) in advanced heart failure with hyponatremia. Teach a daily weight on the same scale and to call for a gain over 2 kg in 3 days, or the client's own threshold.

Count everything liquid at room temperature (ice chips, gelatin, ice cream, soup), split the allowance across the day, use small cups, and ease thirst with mouth care and sugar-free gum or candy.

Worked example. A client with heart failure on a sodium-restricted diet picks lunch: ham sandwich with pickles, canned soup with crackers, grilled chicken with baked potato and salad, or frozen pizza. The chicken plate; cured meat, pickles, canned soup and pizza are top sodium sources.

Renal and carbohydrate-controlled diets

Failing kidneys retain potassium, phosphorus, sodium and water, while protein needs depend on the stage. KDOQI 2020: CKD stages 3–5 not on dialysis, protein 0.55–0.6 g/kg a day (0.6–0.8 with diabetes); on hemodialysis or peritoneal dialysis, 1.0–1.2 g/kg because dialysis removes protein. Sodium under 2.3 g a day. Potassium and phosphorus are adjusted to keep serum levels normal rather than set at one number.

Phosphorus comes from protein foods and, for many people, mostly from additives: look for 'phos' words such as phosphoric acid or disodium phosphate (NIDDK). Binders are taken with meals. Boiling or leaching lowers potassium in some vegetables. Salt substitutes are mostly potassium chloride and can cause hyperkalemia (ODS).

Worked example. A client on hemodialysis wants a snack. Which is best: a banana, a half cup of dried apricots, an apple, or a glass of orange juice? The apple, about 195 mg of potassium, against 422 mg in a banana, 496 mg in a cup of orange juice and 755 mg in half a cup of dried apricots (ODS).

Diabetes: the ADA endorses no single meal plan. Hospitals use carbohydrate-controlled meal plans that count the carbohydrate on each tray so mealtime insulin can match it, with the glucose check, meal and insulin coordinated (ADA 2026). Inpatient goals: 140–180 mg/dL for most critically ill clients and 100–180 mg/dL for others if reached without hypoglycemia; hypoglycemia is below 70, treated with 15 g of fast carbohydrate if the client can swallow.

At home, the plate method uses a 9-inch plate: half nonstarchy vegetables, a quarter protein, a quarter carbohydrate. Choose high-fiber carbohydrate (at least 14 g per 1,000 kcal), replace sugary drinks and juice with water, and keep sodium under 2,300 mg.

Worked example. Rapid-acting insulin is due with lunch, glucose is 165 mg/dL and the tray has not arrived. Hold the dose until the client has the tray and is ready to eat.

GI, gout, celiac, lactose and PKU

Low fiber (low residue) is used briefly in flares of diverticulitis, Crohn disease or ulcerative colitis, after some bowel surgery, and long term for a stricture, often 10–15 g a day (MedlinePlus). Avoid whole grains, popcorn, nuts, seeds, raw vegetables, skins, dried fruit and beans; choose white bread and pasta, peeled cooked vegetables, ripe banana, tender meat and eggs. High fiber (at least 14 g per 1,000 kcal) is raised slowly with more fluid and treats constipation.

Gout: ACR 2020 conditionally recommends limiting alcohol, purines and high-fructose corn syrup, weight loss if overweight, and no vitamin C supplements. Diet changes urate only a little, so drugs stay the mainstay. Meat and seafood raised gout risk and dairy lowered it, while purine-rich vegetables did not (Choi 2004). Limit organ meats, red meat, anchovies, sardines, shellfish, beer and sweet drinks; spinach, asparagus and peas are fine.

Celiac disease needs lifelong gluten avoidance: wheat (durum, semolina, spelt), barley (malt, brewer's yeast), rye and triticale, plus hidden gluten in sauces, soups and sausages (NIDDK). Rice, potatoes, corn, quinoa and buckwheat are safe; oats only if labeled gluten-free (under 20 ppm gluten). Gluten-free diets can run short of fiber, iron and calcium.

Lactose intolerance is not a milk allergy. Many people tolerate 12 g of lactose (about 1 cup of milk); yogurt and hard cheeses are lower in lactose; lactase helps; whey and milk solids mean hidden lactose (NIDDK). Lactose-free and lactose-reduced milk products are as healthy as regular milk, and avoiding all dairy risks low calcium and vitamin D (NIDDK).

PKU is an autosomal recessive enzyme defect found on newborn screening; untreated, phenylalanine causes intellectual disability, seizures, a musty odor and eczema (MedlinePlus Genetics). Treatment is a lifelong low-phenylalanine diet with medical formula, no high-protein foods and no aspartame; ACMG targets blood phenylalanine 120–360 µmol/L. High maternal levels harm the fetus, so women stay on the diet in pregnancy.

Worked example. A client with new celiac disease picks lunch: barley soup, a rice bowl with chicken and vegetables, a malted milkshake or regular oatmeal. The rice bowl.

Protein, calories and food safety

Clients with wounds, cancer, burns or weight loss need more protein and energy. NCI advice: small frequent meals and snacks, high-protein and high-calorie foods, extra protein added to food, and milkshakes, smoothies or soups when solid food is hard to eat.

The strict neutropenic diet has not proved its worth: NCI PDQ cites reviews showing no advantage and a 726-patient transplant study with more infections on it. A 2026 meta-analysis (15 studies, 4,320 patients) found no difference in major infections (RR 1.02); only randomized trials in blood cancer and transplant showed less bacteremia (RR 0.67), so some high-risk units keep limits. The exam answer is safe food handling.

  • Avoid raw or undercooked meat, poultry, fish and eggs, and raw sprouts
  • Avoid unpasteurized milk, juice and cheese made from raw milk
  • Heat deli meats and hot dogs to 165 °F (74 °C) or until steaming (CDC)
  • Avoid refrigerated smoked fish and cut melon left out more than 2 hours (CDC)
  • Wash hands and produce; separate raw meat; refrigerate leftovers promptly

Worked example. A client with an ANC of 400/mm³ is going home. Which statement needs more teaching? 'I will eat deli turkey from the case without heating it.' See Infection Control for the other neutropenic precautions.

Drug–food interactions

MAO in the gut breaks down dietary tyramine. With an MAOI, tyramine reaches the blood, releases norepinephrine and can cause a hypertensive crisis: occipital headache, stiff neck, palpitations, very high blood pressure. In EMSAM studies the tyramine dose that raised systolic pressure 30 mm Hg was 270 mg on the 6 mg patch but 10 mg on tranylcypromine; a high-tyramine meal holds up to 40 mg.

Labels differ. EMSAM needs no diet at 6 mg; at 9 or 12 mg, avoid aged cheeses, aged or fermented sausages, pickled herring, spoiled meat, fava pods, tap beer, yeast extract, sauerkraut and soy products, continuing 2 weeks after lowering or stopping; bottled or canned beer and wine have little or no tyramine (alcohol is still not advised), and fresh processed meats are allowed. NARDIL, an oral MAOI, bans all beer and wine (alcohol-free versions too), yogurt and all cheese except cottage and cream cheese, for 2 weeks after stopping as well. Exam answer: aged cheese, cured sausage, beer, wine, sauerkraut, soy sauce.

Warfarin's label asks for a balanced diet with consistent vitamin K and no drastic changes such as large amounts of leafy greens. Per serving (ODS): cooked collards 530 mcg, turnip greens 426, raw spinach 145, kale 113, broccoli 110. Garlic and ginkgo add bleeding risk; St. John's wort and ginseng weaken warfarin; recheck the INR when any is started or stopped.

Worked example. A client stable on warfarin starts a large daily kale salad. Expect a falling INR. Do not stop the salads: agree on a steady amount, tell the prescriber and recheck the INR. See High-Alert Meds and Mental Health Meds.

Common traps

  • Dialysis clients need more protein than CKD clients not on dialysis.
  • 'No salt added' and 'reduced sodium' are not 'low sodium'.
  • Only about 5% of spinach calcium is absorbed.
  • Plants have no vitamin B12; vegans need fortified foods or a supplement.
  • Lactose-free is not dairy-free: milk allergy needs all milk protein avoided.
  • Warfarin needs steady vitamin K, not none.
  • Purine-rich vegetables are fine in gout.

Exam pearls

  • Safe clear liquids: broth, apple juice, gelatin, popsicles, black coffee or tea.
  • New stroke: NPO, meds included, until the swallow screen.
  • Wet voice at a meal: stop, sit up, suction, report.
  • ASA fasting: 2 h clear, 4 h breast milk, 6 h formula or light meal, 8 h fatty meal.
  • 'Low sodium' is 140 mg or less per serving.
  • Kidney failure: limit potassium, phosphorus, sodium, fluid; binders with meals; no salt substitutes.
  • Hold rapid-acting insulin until the tray arrives.
  • MAOI headache and stiff neck: hypertensive crisis.
