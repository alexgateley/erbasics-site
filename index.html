Head, Eye, Ear, Nose and Throat

The parent of a 7-year-old who has had three nosebleeds this month asks what to do at home the next time one starts. Which instruction should the nurse give?

  1. Lie the child down with a cold pack on the back of the neck
  2. Pack the bleeding nostril with tissue and keep the head upright
  3. Lean forward and pinch the soft lower nose for at least 5 minutes
  4. Tilt the head back and pinch the bony bridge of the nose for 5 minutes

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Cold packs are a familiar remedy but do not compress the bleeding site, and lying flat lets blood run into the pharynx.
  • B. Upright posture helps, but tissue in the nostril does not apply firm pressure and pulling it out can dislodge the clot.
  • C. Correct. Compressing the soft lower third of the nose presses the alae against the anterior septum, where most childhood bleeds start, and leaning forward keeps blood out of the throat.
  • D. A common folk method: pressing the bony bridge misses the bleeding vessels, and tilting back sends blood down the throat where it is swallowed and hides ongoing bleeding.

Most childhood nosebleeds come from Kiesselbach's plexus on the anterior septum, which is controlled by compressing the soft lower third of the nose against the septum. The AAO-HNS guideline advises clearing clots, then firm, sustained compression for at least 5 minutes, continuing to a full 15 minutes if the bleeding slows, with the head flexed slightly forward. Prevention includes saline spray or gel, a bedside humidifier and stopping nose picking and vigorous blowing. Severe or persistent bleeding, or bleeding with weakness or lightheadedness, needs emergency care.

Professional Issues

A 6-year-old is carried into triage 1 hour after his abdomen struck his bicycle handlebar. He is quiet and pale, with cool hands and a capillary refill of 4 seconds, and he says his belly hurts. Data are: HR 152, BP 78/52 mm Hg, RR 28, SpO2 98%. Which ESI level should the triage nurse assign?

  1. ESI 1
  2. ESI 3, as he will need labs, an abdominal CT and IV fluids
  3. ESI 2, as a handlebar injury with abdominal pain is a high-risk situation
  4. ESI 2, as a systolic pressure under 70 mm Hg defines hypotension in a child

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. A systolic pressure of 78 is below his age limit of 82, and with tachycardia and poor skin perfusion he needs immediate fluid and blood resuscitation, a lifesaving intervention.
  • B. He will use many resources, but resource counting at point C is for stable patients, and he is not stable.
  • C. The mechanism is high risk, but the question at point B only applies once point A is ruled out, and his vital signs already show shock.
  • D. 70 mm Hg is the limit for infants; from 1 to 10 years the limit is 70 plus twice the age in years, which is 82 for him.

PALS defines hypotension by age: a systolic pressure under 60 mm Hg in term neonates, under 70 in infants, under 70 plus twice the age in years from 1 to 10 years, and under 90 after age 10. For a 6-year-old the limit is 70 + 2 × 6 = 82 mm Hg, so 78 is hypotension. Children keep their blood pressure up until they are close to collapse, so a low reading with a heart rate over the ESI limit of 120 for his age, pallor and slow capillary refill means shock that is no longer compensated. ESI v5 lists hypotension with signs of hypoperfusion among its level 1 examples and counts significant IV fluid and blood resuscitation as lifesaving interventions, so he goes straight to the resuscitation room.

Gastrointestinal

A 58-year-old at triage says a piece of pork chop stuck in his chest during dinner 90 minutes ago. He is spitting saliva into a cup and cannot swallow a sip of water. Data are: BP 148/88 mm Hg, HR 96/min, RR 18/min, SpO₂ 97% on room air; no stridor. His wife asks whether a fizzy drink would help it go down. Which plan should the nurse anticipate?

  1. IV glucagon, then reassessment in 2 hours before calling endoscopy
  2. Small sips of a carbonated drink to break up the bolus
  3. Emergent endoscopy within hours, with suction ready at the bedside
  4. Endoscopy in the morning after an overnight fast

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Glucagon is sometimes tried, but ESGE says medical treatment should not delay endoscopy; its success rate is low, and the nausea and vomiting it causes can add to the risk of aspiration and perforation.
  • B. The wife's suggestion is popular, but a man who cannot swallow water will pool any liquid above the bolus and may aspirate it.
  • C. Correct. Hypersalivation and inability to swallow liquids signal complete obstruction with a high aspiration risk, and ESGE recommends endoscopy preferably within 2 hours and at the latest within 6 hours.
  • D. A 24-hour window is the ESGE standard for esophageal objects without complete obstruction; a patient who cannot clear his own saliva falls into the emergent group.

ESGE identifies hypersalivation and inability to swallow liquids as signs of complete esophageal obstruction, which carries a high risk of aspiration and calls for emergent therapeutic endoscopy, preferably within 2 hours. Radiographs are not needed for a nonbony food bolus without complications. Most boluses are gently pushed into the stomach, with success over 90%, and more than 75% of patients have an underlying disorder, mainly peptic stricture or eosinophilic esophagitis, so biopsies and follow-up are arranged after removal.

Respiratory

A 6-year-old diagnosed with community-acquired pneumonia 3 days ago has taken oral amoxicillin as prescribed and returns with T 39.4°C (102.9°F), RR 34/min, SpO₂ 93% on room air, and right-sided chest pain. The nurse notes dullness to percussion and decreased breath sounds over the right lower chest. Which intervention should the nurse anticipate FIRST?

  1. Switching to oral azithromycin for atypical pathogens
  2. Increasing the amoxicillin to a higher oral dose
  3. Chest imaging to look for a parapneumonic effusion
  4. An albuterol nebulizer to open the right lower airways

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Atypical organisms are common in school-aged children and can explain a poor response to amoxicillin, but the focal dullness and diminished breath sounds point to a complication that must be imaged before changing drugs.
  • B. Underdosing can cause treatment failure, but the child took the prescribed course and now has signs of a complication, so a dose change without imaging misses the likely cause.
  • C. Correct. A child who is not improving after 48 to 72 hours of therapy needs evaluation of the extent of disease, and dullness with decreased breath sounds suggests fluid that may need drainage.
  • D. Bronchodilators help wheezing from bronchospasm, but decreased breath sounds with dullness reflect fluid or consolidation, which albuterol does not treat.

Most children with community-acquired pneumonia improve within 48 to 72 hours of appropriate antibiotics. The PIDS/IDSA guideline says a child who is not responding by then should be reassessed, including imaging to judge the extent and progression of the pneumonic or parapneumonic process. Dullness to percussion, decreased breath sounds and pleuritic pain on one side are classic signs of a pleural effusion or empyema, a common reason for persistent fever. Small uncomplicated effusions may be treated with antibiotics alone, while moderate effusions with respiratory distress, large effusions and purulent effusions should be drained. The nurse anticipates a chest radiograph or ultrasound, blood work and possibly admission for IV antibiotics and drainage, rather than an empiric change to a different oral antibiotic.
